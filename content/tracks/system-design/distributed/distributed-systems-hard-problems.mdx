    shortAnswer: "Clocks drift, get synced sporadically by NTP, and can jump forward or backward without warning. Two machines' clocks can disagree by hundreds of milliseconds even with NTP. Using timestamps for ordering events across machines is a recipe for silent corruption."
    detailed: |
      Each machine has a clock. They tick slightly different rates (quartz drift). NTP corrects them periodically, sometimes by jumping the clock forward or backward — which means time on one machine isn't monotonically increasing. Leap seconds add another wrinkle.

      The result: machine A might think an event happened 'before' machine B's event, when really they happened in the opposite order on a wall clock. Last-write-wins conflict resolution that relies on timestamps will lose writes.

      Lamport timestamps, vector clocks, and TrueTime (Google Spanner) are responses. Lamport gives logical ordering without physical time. Spanner uses atomic clocks + GPS to bound clock uncertainty to a few milliseconds, then makes transactions wait out the uncertainty window.
    pitfalls:
      - "If your correctness depends on comparing timestamps from different machines, you have a bug waiting to happen."

      Linearizability is the gold standard: reads return the most recent write, no matter which replica answered. From the outside, the system is indistinguishable from one running on a single machine.

## The root of all distributed-systems pain

Single-machine programs have a clean failure model: the process is up and reasonable, or it's down. Cross-machine programs have _partial failure_ — anything can fail independently, and you can't always tell.

When you send a request and don't get a reply, four things are equally consistent with the silence:

1. The request never arrived.
2. It arrived, was processed, but the reply was lost.
3. It arrived, is being processed slowly, and a reply might come.
4. The other node crashed.

From your side, all four look the same. You have to make a decision anyway. That uncertainty is the source of every distributed-systems hardship that follows.

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Partial failure"
  definition="In a distributed system, individual nodes or links can fail independently while others continue. Unlike single-machine systems where everything is up or down together, partial failure leaves the system in ambiguous intermediate states that must be handled explicitly."
>
  Partial failures
</GlossaryTerm>
are what make distributed systems hard. The techniques in this module — timeouts, retries,
idempotency, quorums, consensus — are responses to this one fact.

## The fallacies of distributed computing

Peter Deutsch's classic list of wrong assumptions:

1. The network is reliable.
2. Latency is zero.
3. Bandwidth is infinite.
4. The network is secure.
5. Topology doesn't change.
6. There's one administrator.
7. Transport cost is zero.
8. The network is homogeneous.

Each one feels obvious when listed, but every distributed-systems bug starts with quietly believing one of them. "If the network were reliable, this would work" is the shape of the thought; "the network is not reliable, so we need to handle the case where it isn't" is the shape of the design.

## You can't trust clocks

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Clock skew"
  definition="The difference between two machines' clocks at the same wall-clock moment. Caused by quartz drift, intermittent NTP sync, and leap-second adjustments. Skew is bounded by good ops but can spike to hundreds of milliseconds or more."
>
  Clock skew
</GlossaryTerm>
is the gap between what one machine thinks the time is and what another does. NTP keeps it small
most of the time, but it can spike — and NTP can jump the clock forward or backward, breaking any
code that assumes time moves monotonically.

The consequence: timestamps from different machines cannot reliably order events. A common pattern that breaks: "last write wins" in multi-leader replication. Whoever has the latest timestamp wins — but if their clock is fast by 200ms, they win even if their write was actually earlier.

Google Spanner attacks this with atomic clocks and GPS via _TrueTime_. It returns not a timestamp but an interval `[earliest, latest]` reflecting clock uncertainty, and makes transactions wait out the uncertainty window before committing. That's the kind of engineering required to make timestamps trustworthy across continents.

For everyone else: use logical timestamps (Lamport, vector clocks) when you need ordering, and never compare physical clocks across machines for correctness decisions.

## CAP, correctly stated

<GlossaryTerm
  term="CAP theorem"
  definition="In the presence of a network partition, a distributed system must choose between consistency (linearizability) and availability. It cannot have both. When the network is healthy, both are achievable. CAP is not 'pick two of three' — partition tolerance is mandatory."
>
  CAP
</GlossaryTerm>
is the most misquoted theorem in the field. It does not say "pick two of three". It says: when a
network partition happens, you must choose between consistency and availability. The rest of the
time, you can have both.

- **CP under partition:** refuse operations that can't reach a quorum. ZooKeeper, HBase, Spanner. Consistency preserved; availability suffers (some clients get errors).
- **AP under partition:** accept operations on whatever node is reachable. Cassandra (by default), DynamoDB, Riak. Availability preserved; consistency suffers (reads can return stale data; concurrent writes can conflict).

The right answer depends on what "wrong" means for your data. A bank balance probably wants CP. A shopping cart can tolerate AP (with conflict resolution when partitions heal).

## Linearizability vs eventual consistency

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Linearizability"
  definition="The strongest single-object consistency. The system behaves as if every operation took effect atomically at some point between when it started and ended, against a single copy of the data. Equivalent to 'strong consistency' or 'atomic consistency'."
>
  Linearizability
</GlossaryTerm>
is the gold standard. From the outside, the distributed system behaves as if there were one copy. A
read returns the most recent committed write, period.

It's expensive. In a leader-follower system, reads have to go to the leader (or wait for follower catch-up). In a leaderless system, you need quorum reads and writes. It conflicts with availability under partition.

You need linearizability for:

- **Leader election.** Two leaders is a disaster.
- **Distributed locks.** Two holders defeat the lock.
- **Uniqueness constraints.** Two users with the same email is corruption.

You don't need linearizability for most application data. Feeds can be a little stale. Recommendations can be a little out of date. Analytics rarely need to be current to the second.

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Eventual consistency"
  definition="If no new writes occur, all replicas will eventually converge to the same value. A weak guarantee — it says nothing about how long 'eventually' is, what reads see in the meantime, or how conflicts are resolved. The default for AP systems."
>
  Eventual consistency
</GlossaryTerm>
is the everyday default for AP systems. The contract: if writes stop, replicas converge. Until then,
reads may see stale or conflicting values, and the application has to be okay with that.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Linearizability is a single line at the bank — one teller, one queue, perfect ordering. Eventual
  consistency is many tellers at many branches; eventually they all reconcile their books, but at
  any moment two branches might disagree about your balance. Most apps don't need the single line;
  some absolutely do.
</AnalogyCallout>

## Consensus

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Consensus"
  definition="The problem of getting multiple nodes to agree on a single value (or sequence of values) despite unreliable networks and crashing nodes. Solved by algorithms like Paxos and Raft. Used internally by ZooKeeper, etcd, and strongly-consistent databases."
>
  Consensus
</GlossaryTerm>
sounds simple: get N nodes to agree on one value. The catch is unreliable networks and crashing
nodes. Algorithms (Paxos, Raft) achieve consensus when a majority is alive and reachable; they
refuse to make progress otherwise (preserving safety over liveness — better to wait than to
disagree).

You rarely implement consensus directly. You run ZooKeeper, etcd, or Consul; they become the coordination layer for leader election, distributed locks, and configuration propagation. Strongly-consistent databases (Spanner, CockroachDB, FoundationDB) embed consensus internally.

<WorkedExample title="The bank transfer that almost worked">

**The problem.** Transfer $100 from account A (on database A) to account B (on database B).

**The naive approach.** Subtract $100 from A. Add $100 to B. Each is a local transaction.

**Why it breaks.** Between the two transactions, anything can happen. Database B crashes — A is down $100, B never got it. Database A's reply to "B done" is lost — the application retries the deposit; now B has $200 extra. The network partitions during the second step — you can't tell which side committed.

**The real solution(s).** Two-phase commit (2PC): a coordinator asks both databases to _prepare_ (lock the funds), and if both say yes, asks them to _commit_. If anyone says no or times out, both abort. This works — but the coordinator is itself a single point of failure, and crashes during the second phase leave participants holding locks forever.

Stronger: event-driven sagas with idempotent compensations. Or Spanner-style distributed transactions backed by consensus on every commit. None are free.

**The tradeoff.** Multi-database transactions are expensive and brittle. Many systems sidestep them: put both accounts in the same database, or use eventual consistency with reconciliation (the bank-transfer pattern). The book's broader point: distributed transactions are a tool of last resort, not first.

</WorkedExample>

## What this means in practice

You will not solve consensus from scratch. You will pick libraries (etcd, Raft implementations) and databases (Spanner, CockroachDB, FoundationDB) that have solved it for you. Your job is understanding _where_ you need linearizability and _where_ eventual consistency is fine — because demanding strong guarantees everywhere is expensive, and the price is paid in latency, availability, and operational complexity.

The reflex you want: when reading a distributed system's docs, find the section on partition behavior. Whatever it says about "what happens during a network partition" is the part you'll be paged about.
