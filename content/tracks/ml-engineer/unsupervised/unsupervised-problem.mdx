      Supervised is the well-defined problem: given (x, y) pairs, learn f such that f(x) ≈ y. The loss is computable, generalisation is testable, success is operational.

      Unsupervised is fuzzier. Given only x, the algorithm proposes structure — clusters, principal directions, anomaly scores — and you have to decide whether that structure is useful. There's no loss function that matches what you actually care about, which is why evaluation is the hard part of the field.

      In 2026 the most consequential 'unsupervised' work is pretraining: language models trained on raw text predict the next token (technically self-supervised — labels are inside the data itself).     shortAnswer: "Without labels, there's no ground truth to compare against. Internal metrics (silhouette, inertia) measure something, but not necessarily what matters for your downstream use case."
    detailed: |

      - **Downstream evaluation**: treat the unsupervised output as a feature for a supervised model and measure the supervised model's performance. The honest test in practice.

      The pragmatic position: don't trust unsupervised results in isolation. Plug them into a downstream pipeline and measure what you actually care about.

      **Clustering.** k-means, DBSCAN, hierarchical. Group items by similarity. Production uses: customer segmentation for marketing, image deduplication, finding modes in user behaviour, clustering log lines for incident triage.

<TldrCallout>
  Unsupervised learning is what's left when you take labels away. Three families cover most uses:
  clustering (group similar things), dimensionality reduction (compress while keeping structure),
  and density / anomaly detection (find rare things). The unsolved part of the field is evaluation —
  without labels, "correct" isn't well-defined, and engineers compensate by evaluating downstream
  impact instead.
</TldrCallout>

## The setup

You have data X = (x₁, x₂, ..., x_n) and no labels. No target. No loss function that lines up with what you want. The algorithm has to find something — groups, axes of variation, scores — and you have to decide whether that something is useful.

This is harder than supervised learning, in a specific way: there is no ground truth to measure against. Two clusterings can both look reasonable while disagreeing entirely on which points go together.

## Three families

| Family                   | What it does                            | Canonical methods              | One production use     |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------- |
| Clustering               | Group similar items                     | k-means, DBSCAN, hierarchical  | Customer segmentation  |
| Dimensionality reduction | Compress to fewer dimensions            | PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, autoencoders | Visualising embeddings |
| Density / anomaly        | Score how typical or rare each point is | Isolation Forest, LOF, GMMs    | Fraud detection        |

These families overlap in practice — anomaly detection often uses density estimation, clustering sometimes reduces to a partition of a low-dim embedding — but as a mental partition it works.

## Why evaluation is the hard part

Three options, none entirely satisfactory:

- **Internal metrics.** Silhouette score for clustering, reconstruction error for PCA, etc. They measure structural properties of the algorithm's output, not its usefulness. A clustering with high silhouette can be useless for your downstream task.
- **External metrics.** When you happen to have labels, you can compute adjusted Rand index (clustering similarity to labels) or normalized mutual information. But if you have labels, why aren't you using them in the model?
- **Downstream evaluation.** Treat the unsupervised output as a feature for a supervised model; measure the supervised model's performance. The honest test — and what serious teams do in production.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Supervised learning is like writing essays for a teacher who grades them. Unsupervised learning is
  like organising your bookshelf — there's no objectively correct arrangement, but some arrangements
  help you find books faster than others. The right test is "can I find what I need?", not "does it
  look neat?"
</AnalogyCallout>

## What unsupervised lost, what it kept

Lost: the ambition of "discovering universal structure." In the 1990s and 2000s, unsupervised clustering and dimensionality reduction were proposed as general-purpose preprocessing — find the latent structure first, then do supervised learning on the cleaned representation. The 2010s showed that deep supervised learning (and later self-supervised pretraining) found better representations directly.

Kept: niche but durable production uses.

- **Visualising embeddings.** Every paper showing "the model learned semantic clusters" uses UMAP or t-SNE.
- **Pre-supervised exploration.** Cluster the inputs before modelling to find label sparsity, data slices, outliers.
- **Anomaly detection with no labels.** Fraud, intrusion, manufacturing defects — labels are scarce, unsupervised baselines are competitive.
- **Interpretable feature compression.** When deep models are off the table (regulators, compute), PCA + linear is auditable.

## A note on terminology in 2026

"Unsupervised learning" used to mean classical clustering / PCA / GMMs. Today, "self-supervised learning" — where the labels come from the data itself (next-token prediction, masked image modelling, contrastive learning) — is technically unsupervised in the no-external-labels sense but works very differently.

This module covers classical unsupervised methods. The self-supervised pretraining that drives modern foundation models is its own subject and shows up in Track B (GenAI Builder).
