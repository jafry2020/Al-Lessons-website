importantQuestions:
  - question: "What is a Gaussian Mixture Model?"
    shortAnswer: |
      A probability distribution that's a weighted sum of K Gaussians: p(x) = Σ πₖ · N(x | μₖ, Σₖ). Each Gaussian is a "component"; mixture weights πₖ sum to 1. Used for soft clustering and density estimation.
    detailed: |
      Each component has its own mean μₖ, covariance Σₖ, and mixing weight πₖ. The model defines a probability density everywhere in feature space. For any point x, you can compute:

      - Total density p(x) = Σₖ πₖ · N(x | μₖ, Σₖ).
      - Soft assignment: P(component k | x) = πₖ N(x | μₖ, Σₖ) / p(x) — the responsibility of component k for point x.

      Soft assignment is the key difference from k-means. A point near the boundary of two clusters gets, say, 60% / 40% probabilities for the two components, instead of being hard-assigned to one. This is useful when the data has overlapping clusters.

      GMM with shared spherical covariances and uniform weights reduces to k-means. GMM is k-means with knobs for component shape, size, and overlap.
    pitfalls:
      - "Full covariance matrices have O(d²) parameters per component. For high-d data, use 'diagonal' or 'spherical' covariance to reduce dimensions."
  - question: "How is a GMM fit?"
    shortAnswer: |
      The Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm: alternately compute soft assignments (E-step) and update component parameters as weighted means and covariances (M-step). Monotonically increases the data likelihood.
    detailed: |
      EM (Dempster, Laird, Rubin, 1977):

      **E-step:** with current parameters, compute the responsibility of each component for each point (probability the point came from that component).

      **M-step:** update each component's mean as the responsibility-weighted average of points; covariance as the responsibility-weighted covariance; mixing weight as the average responsibility.

      Both steps monotonically increase the data log-likelihood (this is provable). The algorithm converges to a local maximum — same caveat as k-means. Multiple random starts and pick the best.

      EM is the general framework for fitting models with latent variables. Many algorithms — GMM, HMM, factor analysis — are special cases. Modern deep generative models (VAEs, diffusion) use variational generalisations of the same idea.
    pitfalls:
      - "EM converges to a local maximum. Initialise with k-means centroids for stable results — most libraries do this by default."
  - question: "How do you pick the number of components K?"
    shortAnswer: |
      BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) is the standard. It rewards higher data likelihood and penalises more parameters. Sweep K, pick the lowest BIC. Held-out log-likelihood works too.
    detailed: |
      Training log-likelihood always improves with more components — eventually you'd have one Gaussian per training point. To pick K honestly:

      - **BIC** = -2 · log L + p · log n, where L is the likelihood, p the number of parameters, n the dataset size. Lower is better. Penalises model complexity proportionally to log n.
      - **AIC** = -2 · log L + 2p. Weaker complexity penalty; tends to over-select components.
      - **Held-out log-likelihood**. Train on a subset, evaluate likelihood on a held-out set. The honest test.
      - **Dirichlet Process GMM** (sklearn's `BayesianGaussianMixture`). Lets the model "choose" K by including a prior that suppresses unused components. Effectively non-parametric.

      In practice for production, BIC is the standard quick choice; held-out likelihood for serious work.
    pitfalls:
      - "BIC assumes the model family is correct. If your data isn't a mixture of Gaussians, BIC may not pick the most useful K."
  - question: "When is a GMM the right tool?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Soft clustering on tabular data with roughly Gaussian-shaped clusters. Density estimation for anomaly detection (low p(x) = anomaly). Initialising downstream models (clustering before supervised learning).
    detailed: |
      Concrete production uses in 2026:

      - **Soft clustering** when you need probabilistic assignments (mixed-membership users, biology, voice activity detection).
      - **Density-based anomaly detection.** Fit GMM on normal data, flag points with very low p(x). Often competitive with Isolation Forest on tabular data.
      - **Mixture models for downstream tasks.** E.g., model a regression target as conditional GMM for capturing multimodal outputs (mixture density networks).
      - **Initialising k-means or downstream methods** when soft assignment is useful.

      Where GMM doesn't fit: image / text / audio (the Gaussian assumption is wrong for raw representations), very high-dim data without preprocessing, when k-means is sufficient.
    pitfalls:
      - "GMMs scale O(n·K·d²) per iteration with full covariance. Use diagonal covariance for d > 50."
  - question: "What's kernel density estimation, briefly?"
    shortAnswer: |
      A non-parametric density estimate: place a small kernel (usually Gaussian) on every training point and average. Smooth and flexible, but scales poorly and requires bandwidth tuning.
    detailed: |
      KDE: p̂(x) = (1/n) Σᵢ K_h(x − xᵢ), where K_h is a kernel (Gaussian, Epanechnikov) with bandwidth h.

      Pros: makes no parametric assumption about distribution shape. The density adapts to the data.

      Cons: O(n) cost per evaluation (every training point contributes). Bandwidth h must be tuned and dominates the result. Curse of dimensionality — KDE needs exponentially more data as d grows.

      Use cases: 1D or 2D density visualisation (alternative to histograms — `seaborn.kdeplot`), anomaly detection in low-dim feature spaces, smooth interpolation of empirical distributions.

      In 2026 production: mostly visualisation. For density-based anomaly detection on tabular data, GMM or Isolation Forest scale much better.
    pitfalls:
      - "Bandwidth selection (Scott's rule, Silverman's rule) is essential and frequently bad. Cross-validate when stakes are high."

  detection, and as conceptual closure on how EM and probabilistic clustering work.
</TldrCallout>

## What a GMM is

A Gaussian Mixture Model is a distribution made of K Gaussians:

p(x) = π₁ · N(x | μ₁, Σ₁) + π₂ · N(x | μ₂, Σ₂) + ... + π_K · N(x | μ_K, Σ_K)

with mixing weights πₖ summing to 1. Each component k has its own mean μₖ and covariance Σₖ.

Two interpretations, both useful:

1. **Soft clustering.** Each point belongs to every component with some probability. A point at the border of two components might be 70% / 30%.
2. **Density estimation.** The model gives you p(x) anywhere — useful for anomaly detection (low p(x) = anomalous) and likelihood-based comparisons.

## GMM vs k-means

Both partition the data into clusters. The differences:

| Aspect                 | k-means               | GMM                                            |
| ---------------------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Assignment             | Hard (one cluster)    | Soft (probabilities)                           |
| Cluster shape          | Spherical (Euclidean) | Ellipsoidal (covariance allows tilt and scale) |
| Output                 | Cluster IDs           | Probabilities + density p(x)                   |
| Parameters per cluster | mean only             | mean + covariance + weight                     |
| Training               | Lloyd iteration       | EM algorithm                                   |
| Sensitive to init      | Yes                   | Yes (initialise with k-means)                  |

For roughly spherical, balanced clusters with clean boundaries: k-means is fine. For overlapping clusters, elongated components, or when you need probabilities: GMM.

## EM in one paragraph

Expectation-Maximisation alternates two steps:

- **E-step.** With current parameters, compute the responsibility of each component for each point (the probability the point came from that component).
- **M-step.** Update each component's mean, covariance, and weight from the responsibilities (weighted averages).

Both steps monotonically increase the data log-likelihood, so the algorithm converges. To a local maximum — multiple random inits required. `sklearn.mixture.GaussianMixture` initialises with k-means centroids by default.

EM (Dempster, Laird, Rubin, 1977) is the general algorithm for fitting models with latent variables. Many algorithms — GMM, HMM, factor analysis, even modern variational methods — are special cases or generalisations.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Imagine a school with several classes you can't directly see. You watch students arrive at the
  building. You guess which class each belongs to from their backpack (E-step). Then you update your
  stereotype of each class based on the students you assigned to it (M-step). You re-guess each
  student's class given your updated stereotypes. Repeat. You arrive at consistent stereotypes and
  consistent guesses — but only one of many possible consistent answers.
</AnalogyCallout>

## Picking K with BIC

Likelihood always improves with more components — at K = n you have a Gaussian per training point and infinite likelihood. To pick K honestly, penalise complexity:

BIC = -2 · log L + p · log n

where L is the likelihood, p the parameter count, n the dataset size. Lower BIC is better. Sweep K, plot BIC, pick the minimum.

For modest data, BIC tends to be conservative; for very large data it can over-select. Held-out log-likelihood is the most direct test if you can afford the data split.

## Where GMMs earn their place in 2026

- **Soft clustering** when probabilistic membership matters (mixed-membership models, voice activity detection, biology).
- **Density-based anomaly detection.** Fit on normal data; low p(x) = anomaly. Competitive with Isolation Forest on tabular.
- **Mixture density networks** (Bishop, 1994). Output a GMM from a neural network — useful when the target distribution is multimodal (robot trajectory prediction, future video frame prediction).
- **Speaker / source separation** in audio (older but still used).
- **Pedagogically**, as the canonical example of latent-variable models — the bridge to VAEs and modern probabilistic deep learning.

Where they're not the right tool:

- Image, text, audio raw representations (Gaussian assumption wrong).
- Very high-dim data without dimensionality reduction (covariance matrices blow up).
- Anywhere k-means is sufficient (cheaper).

## A note on kernel density estimation

KDE is the non-parametric alternative: place a small Gaussian on every training point, average. Get a smooth density estimate without assuming any parametric form.

p̂(x) = (1/n) Σᵢ K_h(x − xᵢ)

Pros: very flexible. Cons: O(n) per evaluation, bandwidth h must be tuned, scales terribly with dimensions.

In 2026 production, KDE is mostly used for 1D / 2D visualisations (`seaborn.kdeplot`, `matplotlib.contour`). For density-based anomaly detection on real data, GMM or Isolation Forest are more practical.
