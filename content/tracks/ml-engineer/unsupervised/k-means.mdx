importantQuestions:
  - question: "What is the k-means objective and what does the algorithm actually minimise?"
    shortAnswer: "Inertia: the sum of squared distances from each point to its assigned cluster centroid. k-means alternates between assigning points to nearest centroid and recomputing centroids, monotonically decreasing inertia."
    detailed: |
      Formally: minimise Σᵢ ‖xᵢ − μ_c(i)‖² over both the assignment c(·) and the centroids μ_k. This is NP-hard in general; k-means (Lloyd's algorithm, 1957) uses a greedy coordinate-descent heuristic:

      1. **Assign**: for each point, set c(i) = argmin_k ‖xᵢ − μ_k‖². Inertia goes down or stays the same.
      2. **Update**: for each k, set μ_k = mean of points with c(i) = k. Inertia goes down or stays the same.
      3. Repeat until assignments stop changing.

      Both steps monotonically decrease inertia, so the algorithm converges. But to a local minimum — not necessarily the global one. Run multiple times with different inits and keep the best. `sklearn.cluster.KMeans(n_init=10)` does this by default.
    pitfalls:
      - "Inertia is monotonically decreasing in k. You can't pick k by minimising it; you'd pick k=n and assign each point its own cluster."
  - question: "What are the four assumptions k-means makes that often fail?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Spherical clusters, equal cluster variance, equal cluster size, isotropic feature space (Euclidean distance is meaningful). Real data violates at least one of these almost always; the question is how badly.
    detailed: |
      **Spherical clusters.** k-means assigns by Euclidean distance to a centroid, which implicitly assumes clusters are round blobs. Crescent-shaped or elongated clusters get split or merged incorrectly. (Try moons or rings — k-means fails comically.)

      **Equal variance.** Two clusters with very different spreads will be cut in a place that minimises inertia overall, not at the natural boundary. The compact cluster wins.

      **Equal size.** k-means tends to balance cluster sizes because inertia is sensitive to point counts. A 1000-point cluster and a 50-point cluster often get fitted with the large cluster split in half.

      **Isotropic features (Euclidean distance).** If features are on different scales or correlated, Euclidean distance is dominated by the largest-scale feature. Always standardise; consider Mahalanobis distance for correlated features.

      When these fail badly, switch to DBSCAN (no spherical assumption), Gaussian mixture models (allows different variances), or do PCA / autoencoder embedding first and cluster the embedding.
    pitfalls:
      - "Forgetting to standardise is the single most common k-means bug."
  - question: "How do you pick k?"
    shortAnswer: "Three heuristics: elbow on the inertia curve, silhouette score, gap statistic. All are heuristics — they suggest, they don't decide. If you have a downstream metric, use it."
    detailed: |
      **Elbow method.** Plot inertia vs. k. Pick the k where the rate of decrease 'elbows.' Subjective, often ambiguous on real data.

      **Silhouette score.** For each point, measure how similar it is to its own cluster vs. the nearest other cluster. Average across points. Higher is better; ranges from −1 to 1. More objective than the elbow but biased toward k that produces well-separated spherical clusters.

      **Gap statistic.** Compare actual inertia to what you'd get from random data. Picks k where the gap (improvement over random) is largest. More principled, slower to compute.

      **Downstream metric.** If you'll use the clustering for a supervised task (e.g., cluster ID as a feature), measure the supervised model's accuracy across different k. This is the most honest test.

      For business segmentation, the right k is often dictated by the team that will act on it: 'we can run 5 different campaigns' → k=5.
    pitfalls:
      - "Don't trust the elbow method on small datasets — the curve is too noisy to see a clear elbow."
  - question: "What's the difference between k-means and k-means++?"
    shortAnswer: "k-means is the iteration (assign-update loop). k-means++ is a smarter initialisation that spreads initial centroids out, leading to better local minima and faster convergence. Always use k-means++."
    detailed: |
      Random initialisation can land two centroids next to each other or all in one cluster, leading to poor local optima. k-means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007) picks initial centroids probabilistically — each new centroid is chosen with probability proportional to squared distance from the nearest existing centroid. The result: centroids start spread out, the algorithm converges faster, and the final inertia is closer to the global minimum.

      `sklearn.cluster.KMeans` uses `init='k-means++'` by default. Don't override it. The compute cost of init is negligible compared to the iterations.
    pitfalls:
      - "Even with k-means++, run multiple inits (n_init=10) and keep the best. Init is randomised; one bad seed can still ruin a run."
  - question: "When does k-means actually deploy in 2026?"
    shortAnswer: "As a fast preprocessing step (vector quantisation, feature bucketing) and as a baseline. Not as the final clustering for serious segmentation — use DBSCAN, HDBSCAN, or embedding-then-cluster."
    detailed: |
      Production uses where k-means still wins:

      - **Vector quantisation** for nearest-neighbour indexes (IVF in Faiss is k-means on the database). Speeds up ANN search dramatically.
      - **Bucketing high-cardinality categoricals** (assign each item to a cluster of items, use cluster as a feature).
      - **Image colour palette extraction** (k-means in RGB space). A classical use that still works.
      - **Initialising a more sophisticated method** — GMM, hierarchical, even some deep clustering methods bootstrap from k-means centroids.

      For interpretable customer segmentation, the practical answer in 2026 is often: embed users with a learned model (or a simple aggregation), cluster the embedding with HDBSCAN or k-means, then have a human label each cluster. The clustering step is a small part of the pipeline; the embedding does the heavy lifting.
    pitfalls:
      - "Mini-batch k-means scales to millions of points. Use it for large datasets — full k-means becomes a memory bottleneck."

<TldrCallout>
  k-means partitions n points into k clusters by alternating between assigning points to the nearest
  centroid and recomputing centroids as the mean of their assigned points. Cheap, fast, ubiquitous —
  and quietly assumes your clusters are spherical, equally sized, and equally spread. Master the
  algorithm and its assumptions; reach for it as a baseline and a building block, not a final answer
  for serious segmentation.
</TldrCallout>

## The algorithm in three lines

1. Pick k initial centroids (k-means++ spreads them out).
2. Assign each point to its nearest centroid.
3. Move each centroid to the mean of its assigned points. Go to 2.

Stop when assignments don't change. That's it. Lloyd's algorithm (1957) is one of the oldest still-relevant ML algorithms.

The objective being minimised is **inertia**: `Σᵢ ‖xᵢ − μ_{c(i)}‖²`. Each step monotonically decreases inertia, so the algorithm converges — to a local minimum.

## The four assumptions

k-means looks innocent. The assumptions are buried in the Euclidean distance:

| Assumption                  | What breaks when violated                 |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Clusters are spherical      | Moons, rings, elongated shapes get split  |
| Equal cluster variance      | Tight cluster captures most assignments   |
| Equal cluster size          | Big clusters get split; small ones merged |
| Isotropic (scaled) features | Largest-scale feature dominates distance  |

The fix for the last is always: standardise features before k-means. The first three require a different algorithm (DBSCAN, hierarchical, or GMM — covered in A3.3 and A3.8) or a different feature space (cluster a PCA / autoencoder embedding instead of raw features).

<AnalogyCallout>
  k-means is a lawn sprinkler placement problem: you want k sprinklers positioned to minimise the
  total distance from any patch of grass to its nearest sprinkler. Move each sprinkler to the centre
  of the area it currently covers; reassign each patch to its nearest sprinkler; repeat. The
  algorithm finds a sensible arrangement — but it can't help if your lawn is shaped like a long thin
  strip.
</AnalogyCallout>

## Picking k

The number of clusters is a hyperparameter, and it has no clean answer. Three heuristics:

- **Elbow method.** Plot inertia vs k. Pick the k where additional clusters stop helping much. Subjective; the elbow is often unclear.
- **Silhouette score.** For each point, ratio of within-cluster similarity to nearest-other-cluster similarity. Average across all points. Higher is better. Cleaner than the elbow but still a heuristic.
- **Downstream metric.** If clustering feeds a supervised model, optimise k against the supervised model's accuracy. The most honest test.

In business problems the "right" k often comes from outside the data: "we can run five campaigns" → k=5. The clustering then becomes the right grouping for _that_ k, not the underlying truth.

## k-means in real pipelines

In 2026, the production places k-means actually lives:

- **Vector quantisation in nearest-neighbour search.** Faiss's IVF index clusters the database into k Voronoi cells with k-means, then only searches the relevant cell at query time. Massive speedups for billion-scale vector search.
- **Image palette extraction.** k-means in RGB space gives k "representative colours."
- **Bucketing high-cardinality features.** Replace 100,000 user IDs with their cluster ID (1 of k) for a tabular model.
- **Initialising fancier methods.** GMMs, hierarchical clustering, even some deep clustering bootstrap from k-means centroids.

What it's _not_ great for: high-stakes customer segmentation, anomaly detection, anything where the data is non-blob-shaped. For those, the modern pipeline embeds the data first (via a supervised or self-supervised model) and clusters the embedding with HDBSCAN.

## Cost and scale

For n points, k clusters, d dimensions, T iterations: O(n·k·d·T) per run. For large n, use **mini-batch k-means** — `sklearn.cluster.MiniBatchKMeans` — which approximates the iteration on small random batches. Sub-linear memory in n, slightly worse final inertia, scales to millions of points easily.

For billion-scale (vector databases, recommendation systems), the implementations in Faiss and ScaNN are heavily engineered to use GPU and product quantisation. The textbook algorithm is the same; the production code is years of optimisation.
