## The maximum-margin idea

Among all hyperplanes that separate two linearly separable classes, pick the one whose distance to the nearest point on either side is largest. This is the **margin**.

The optimisation: minimise ‖w‖² subject to yᵢ(xᵢᵀw + b) ≥ 1 for all i. The reciprocal of ‖w‖ is the margin, so minimising ‖w‖² maximises the margin. For non-separable data, soft-margin SVMs introduce slack variables and a hyperparameter C that trades off margin width against misclassification:

minimise (1/2)‖w‖² + C Σᵢ max(0, 1 − yᵢ(xᵢᵀw + b))

The second term is the **hinge loss**. C controls regularisation: high C = small margin, fit training data hard; low C = wide margin, tolerate misclassifications.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Picture the data as red and blue tokens on a table. Find the widest plank of wood you can wedge
  between them without tipping any tokens. The plank's centre line is the boundary; the plank's
  width is the margin. Only the tokens touching the plank matter — those are the support vectors.
</AnalogyCallout>

## The kernel trick — in one paragraph

The whole SVM machinery only ever uses inner products xᵢᵀxⱼ between training points. Replace every such inner product with a function K(xᵢ, xⱼ) and you get a classifier in a new feature space, without ever computing the features. The **RBF kernel** K(x, x') = exp(−γ‖x − x'‖²) corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature space. The **polynomial kernel** corresponds to all degree-d feature combinations. The **linear kernel** is just the original inner product (no trick — and it's what you'd usually want).

This was revolutionary in the 1990s. Today: explicit non-linear features come from neural networks; tree ensembles capture interactions automatically; both scale better than kernel SVMs.

## Why SVMs lost their dominance

Three reasons:

1. **They don't scale.** Training is O(n²) to O(n³) for kernel SVMs. Above ~10⁵ examples it's impractical.
2. **No native probability output.** SVMs output signed distances; you have to fit a logistic on top (Platt scaling) for calibrated probabilities. Logistic regression gives you this directly.
3. **Better alternatives emerged.** Boosted trees beat SVMs on tabular data. Deep learning beats SVMs on text and images. Both scale where SVMs don't.

In 2026, SVMs survive in niches: small bioinformatics datasets, high-dimensional sparse text classification on tight budgets, baseline comparisons in academic papers. That's about it.

| Setting                          | Use SVM?                  | Better choice             |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| n < 5k, sparse high-dim features | Reasonable                | Linear SVM, or LR         |
| Tabular, n > 10k                 | No                        | XGBoost / LightGBM        |
| Text, n > 10k                    | No                        | Transformer + linear head |
| Image                            | No                        | CNN or ViT                |
| Need calibrated probabilities    | No (unless Platt scaling) | Logistic regression       |

## What to remember

The geometric framing — maximum margin, support vectors, the kernel trick — is one of the most elegant ideas in classical ML. It's why SVMs are in every textbook. But:

- The theoretical generalisation bounds underperformed expectations in practice.
- The scaling limits are real.
- Newer methods (gradient boosting, deep learning) cover SVMs' use cases with better practical properties.

Learn the geometry; recognise the kernel trick when you see it (it appears in Gaussian processes, kernel PCA, and in some attention-related theory); reach for SVMs only in their small remaining niche.

Vapnik's _Statistical Learning Theory_ (1995) and the original SVM papers are still worth reading for the elegance of the formulation. As a deployment choice in 2026, they rarely beat the alternatives.
