  - question: "How does kNN make a prediction?"
    shortAnswer: "For a new point, find the k nearest training examples (by some distance metric). For classification, take the majority vote of their labels. For regression, average their target values."
    detailed: |
      No training in the traditional sense — kNN stores the entire training set and defers all work to inference time. This makes it a 'lazy learner.' The hyperparameters: k (number of neighbours), the distance metric (Euclidean is default; cosine is standard for embeddings), and optionally a weighting scheme that weights closer neighbours more.

      kNN is the simplest example of a non-parametric model: the number of effective parameters grows with n, the dataset size. There are no weights to fit; the data is the model.
    pitfalls:
      - "Memory cost: you must store every training example. For millions of examples and high-dimensional vectors, this is heavy."
  - question: "How does k trade off bias and variance?"
    shortAnswer: "Small k = low bias, high variance (single noisy neighbour can flip a prediction). Large k = high bias, low variance (predictions smooth toward the global majority). Tune k on validation."
    detailed: |
      k=1 gives zero training error and a decision boundary that exactly traces the training set — pure memorisation. k=n (the whole dataset) predicts the global majority class everywhere — pure underfit. The sweet spot is in between, typically k = 5 to 30 depending on dataset size.

      A useful default: try odd k values to avoid ties, choose by validation accuracy. For small datasets, leave-one-out cross-validation is feasible since kNN training is free.
    pitfalls:
      - "Even k can produce ties in binary classification. Use odd k or distance-weighted voting."
  - question: "Why is distance metric so important?"
    shortAnswer: "kNN is entirely defined by 'nearness.' Different metrics produce different neighbours, hence different predictions. Euclidean assumes isotropic features; cosine assumes direction matters more than magnitude (standard for embeddings)."
    detailed: |
      Euclidean: standard for low-dim numerical data. Sensitive to feature scale — standardise first.

      Cosine: measures angle between vectors. Standard for text embeddings, image embeddings, anything where 'direction in feature space' matches semantic similarity better than absolute coordinates. Insensitive to vector magnitude.

      Manhattan (L1): less sensitive to outliers than Euclidean. Useful when features have very different scales after standardisation.

      Mahalanobis: accounts for feature correlations. Effectively the distance after a whitening transform.

      For modern embedding-based search, cosine similarity is almost universal — most embedding models are trained with a cosine-similarity objective.
    pitfalls:
      - "Cosine similarity is 1 − cosine distance. Don't confuse them in library APIs."
  - question: "Why does kNN break down in high dimensions?"
    shortAnswer: "The 'curse of dimensionality.' In high-dim space, all points become roughly equidistant — the nearest neighbour isn't meaningfully closer than the farthest. Distance-based methods lose their signal."
    detailed: |
      Sample n random points uniformly in d dimensions. The ratio of farthest-to-nearest distance from any query point tends to 1 as d grows. Distance stops differentiating neighbours from non-neighbours.

      The fix is not to use kNN on raw high-dimensional features, but to first project into a lower-dimensional embedding where 'distance' has semantic meaning. For text: sentence-transformers, OpenAI embeddings, etc. For images: CLIP, DINO. The embedding compresses what matters; kNN over the embedding works beautifully even though kNN over raw pixels or raw token counts would not.

      This is the move that underlies modern retrieval systems.
    pitfalls:
      - "Even good embeddings can have noise dimensions. Dimensionality reduction (PCA, UMAP) sometimes helps further."
  - question: "How does this connect to RAG and vector databases?"
    shortAnswer: "Modern RAG retrieves relevant documents via k-nearest-neighbour search over embeddings. The 'k' in 'top-k retrieval' is literally the k of kNN."

    shortAnswer: "Two main places: retrieval (vector search at the heart of RAG, recommender systems, image search) and small low-stakes tabular problems where you want a quick non-linear baseline."
    detailed: |
      As a classifier on tabular data, kNN is a baseline only — boosted trees outperform it. But as a retrieval primitive over learned embeddings, it powers a huge fraction of production AI:
      - RAG (chunks indexed in pgvector, served via Postgres)
      - Recommender systems (user/item embeddings, retrieve nearest items)
      - Image search (CLIP embeddings + Faiss)
      - Deduplication and clustering (find near-duplicates by embedding distance)

<TldrCallout>
  k-Nearest neighbours predicts a new example by looking at its k closest training examples. It has
  no training phase — the data is the model. Conceptually obsolete as a tabular classifier;
  conceptually central in 2026 as the retrieval primitive behind every RAG pipeline, recommender
  system, and embedding-based search.
</TldrCallout>

## The algorithm

1. Store all training examples (xᵢ, yᵢ).
2. For a query x, compute distance d(x, xᵢ) to each training point.
3. Find the k smallest distances; collect their labels.
4. Predict the majority label (classification) or mean target (regression).

There is no fitting. The "training" cost is O(1); the inference cost is O(n·d) per query for exact search. This trade-off matters at scale.

## Distance is everything

kNN is defined by the distance metric. Different metrics → different neighbours → different predictions. Three you should know:

| Metric    | Formula                     | When to use                           |
| --------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Euclidean | √Σ(xᵢ − x'ᵢ)²               | Low-dim numerical data, after scaling |
| Cosine    | 1 − (x · x') / (‖x‖ · ‖x'‖) | Embeddings, text, image features      |
| Manhattan | Σ                           | xᵢ − x'ᵢ                              |

For embedding-based retrieval, cosine is the default — embedding models are typically trained to make semantically similar things have small cosine distance.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Euclidean asks "how far apart are these two points on the map?" Cosine asks "do they face the same
  direction from the origin?" For semantic embeddings, direction is the right question; magnitude
  often encodes confidence or document length, which you don't want to factor into similarity.
</AnalogyCallout>

## The curse of dimensionality

In high-dimensional spaces, distance stops differentiating points. Sample n random vectors in 1000 dimensions: the ratio of farthest-to-nearest distance from any query is close to 1. Every neighbour is "about the same distance" away, which means the nearest neighbour isn't meaningfully closer than a random one.

The way modern systems get past this: don't run kNN on raw high-dim features. Embed first. A 768-dimensional sentence embedding from a pretrained model has structure — semantically similar sentences are genuinely closer — that raw 50,000-dimensional bag-of-words does not. The embedding compresses what matters; kNN over the embedding works.

## kNN is the algorithm behind RAG

A modern RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) pipeline:

1. **Index time.** Chunk a corpus. Embed each chunk with a model like `text-embedding-3-large` or a sentence-transformer. Store the vectors in a database — Faiss, pgvector (Postgres), Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Chroma, ScaNN.
2. **Query time.** Embed the user query. Retrieve the top-k chunks by cosine similarity. Concatenate them into the LLM prompt.

Step 2 is kNN. The "k" in "top-k retrieval" is literally the k of this lesson. The interesting engineering is in the embeddings and the index (approximate nearest neighbours via HNSW or IVF-PQ for sub-millisecond queries on billions of vectors), but the underlying model is what you just learned.

The same algorithm powers:

- **Recommender systems.** User and item embeddings; retrieve nearest items per user.
- **Image search.** CLIP embeddings; retrieve images closest to a query image or text.
- **Deduplication.** Near-duplicate detection by embedding distance.
- **Few-shot classification.** Embed the query; retrieve the nearest labelled examples; predict their label.

## Approximate kNN: how scale is achieved

Exact kNN on a billion vectors is impractical. Production systems use **approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN)** search:

- **HNSW** (Hierarchical Navigable Small World, Malkov & Yashunin 2016) — graph-based, the default in Faiss, pgvector, Weaviate, Qdrant. High recall, low latency.
- **IVF** (Inverted File) — cluster vectors into Voronoi cells, search only the relevant cells. Often combined with **product quantisation (PQ)** to compress vectors. Used in Faiss for very large indexes.
- **ScaNN** (Google) — anisotropic quantisation, optimised for inner product search.

These return the _approximate_ k nearest neighbours — close enough for retrieval, much faster than exact search. Measure recall@k on your data before deploying.

## The bottom line

| Use case                                  | Model of choice                     |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Tabular classification baseline           | Logistic regression / XGBoost       |
| Tabular regression baseline               | Linear regression / XGBoost         |
| **Retrieve relevant chunks for RAG**      | **Approximate kNN over embeddings** |
| **Find similar items for recommendation** | **Approximate kNN over embeddings** |
| Few-shot classification on embeddings     | kNN (with k=1 to 5)                 |

kNN as a tabular classifier: nice idea for intuition, rarely deployed. kNN as a vector-search primitive: everywhere.
