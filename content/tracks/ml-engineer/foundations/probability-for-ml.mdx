importantQuestions:
  - question: "What is a random variable?"
    shortAnswer: "A quantity whose value depends on the outcome of some random process. Roll of a die, height of a person picked from a crowd, whether an email is spam. In ML, almost everything observed is a random variable."
    detailed: |
      A random variable is a function that assigns a number (or category) to the outcome of a random experiment. 'Will it rain tomorrow' is a random variable with values {yes, no}; 'tomorrow's high temperature' is a random variable with values in real numbers.

      ML treats data points, predictions, and even model parameters as random variables. The training set is a sample of random variables; the model's predictions are random variables; the model's loss is a random variable that depends on which data points it sees. Embracing this framing — that everything is a sample from a distribution — is the doorway into modern ML theory.
    pitfalls:
      - "Random doesn't mean uniform. A 'random' variable can have any distribution — heavily skewed, multi-modal, anything."
      - "The 'variable' part of the name is misleading; in math it's a function, not a variable."
  - question: "What is a probability distribution?"
    shortAnswer: "A description of how a random variable's values are spread out — which values are likely, which are rare. For discrete variables it's a table of probabilities; for continuous variables it's a probability density function."
    detailed: |
      For a coin, the distribution is simple: P(heads) = 0.5, P(tails) = 0.5. For a die: each face has P = 1/6. These are *discrete* distributions — probabilities for each possible value.

      For continuous quantities like 'height of an adult', there are infinitely many possible values, so we describe the *density* instead. The Gaussian (or 'normal') distribution is the famous bell curve — it's the most common density in nature and in ML.

      The defining property of any distribution: probabilities sum (or integrate) to exactly 1 over all possible outcomes. If they don't, you don't have a distribution.
    pitfalls:
      - "A density value can be greater than 1 — densities aren't probabilities. They become probabilities only when integrated over an interval."
      - "Real-world data rarely follows a textbook distribution exactly. Models that assume Gaussian-ness can fail badly on long-tailed data."
  - question: "What is conditional probability?"
    shortAnswer: "The probability of one event given that another event happened. Written P(A|B), read as 'probability of A given B'. P(A) is unconditional; P(A|B) is conditional on B being true."
    detailed: |
      Suppose 1% of emails are spam — P(spam) = 0.01. But 60% of emails containing the word 'lottery' are spam — P(spam | 'lottery') = 0.6. The presence of 'lottery' is a strong conditioning event; it dramatically raises our probability estimate.

      Conditional probability is the language ML uses to express predictions. A classifier doesn't just say 'spam' — internally it computes P(spam | features of this email). Most modern classifiers output exactly this kind of conditional probability.
    pitfalls:
      - "P(A|B) is generally not equal to P(B|A). Conflating these is the most common probability error in everyday reasoning — and the reason Bayes' rule exists."
  - question: "What is Bayes' rule, and why does ML love it?"
    shortAnswer: "Bayes' rule connects P(A|B) and P(B|A): P(A|B) = P(B|A) · P(A) / P(B). It's the formula for updating a belief P(A) in light of new evidence B. Almost every probabilistic ML model can be framed as Bayesian inference."
    detailed: |
      The rule is exact, not a heuristic. Here's why it matters:

      You have a prior belief about something — P(A), how likely A is before you see evidence. You observe some evidence B. Bayes' rule tells you the posterior probability P(A|B) — how likely A is *now*, given B. The update involves two ingredients: how likely B was under A (the likelihood), and how likely A was to begin with (the prior).

      In ML: A is 'the model parameters take some value', B is 'the data you observed'. Training is the process of updating beliefs about A from B. Modern deep learning doesn't compute exact Bayesian updates — it does something cruder but related — but the framing illuminates what's happening.
    pitfalls:
      - "Bayes' rule does not say 'always update your belief' — it says 'here's how to do it correctly when you do'. Bad priors and bad likelihoods give bad posteriors."
      - "P(B) in the denominator is often hard to compute exactly, which is why approximate Bayesian methods (variational inference, MCMC) are an entire subfield."
  - question: "What does it mean for two events to be independent?"
    shortAnswer: "Knowing one doesn't change the probability of the other. Formally: P(A|B) = P(A), or equivalently P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B). Independence is a strong assumption and is often violated in practice."
    detailed: |
      Two coin flips are independent: the second is 50/50 regardless of what the first was. Whether two emails are spam is *not* independent: spam tends to come in bursts from the same sender. Whether two pixels in an image are bright is not independent: nearby pixels are correlated.

      Many ML algorithms assume independence to keep the math tractable — naive Bayes is named after this assumption. The assumption is often wrong, and naive Bayes still works surprisingly well in practice, which is a humbling fact about the field.
    pitfalls:
      - "'Naive Bayes' is naive because it pretends features are independent given the label. Despite the obvious wrongness, it's a competitive baseline for text classification."
  - question: "What's the difference between a likelihood and a probability?"
    shortAnswer: "A probability is P(data | parameters): how likely the data is, given some fixed parameter values. A likelihood is the same formula viewed as a function of the parameters with data fixed. Same math, different perspective — but the terminology is strict."
    detailed: |
      Imagine you flip a coin 10 times and see 7 heads. If the coin is fair (P(heads) = 0.5), the probability of seeing 7 heads is some number. If the coin has P(heads) = 0.7, the probability is some other number. As you vary the assumed parameter, you get a curve. *That curve is the likelihood function.*

      Training a model often means finding parameters that maximize the likelihood — this is *maximum likelihood estimation* (MLE), one of the most-used ideas in classical statistics and a foundation of how neural networks are trained.
    pitfalls:
      - "Don't write 'the likelihood is 0.42' as if it were a probability. Likelihoods can exceed 1 (for continuous data) and only carry information by ratio."
  - question: "If two diseases have base rates of 1% and 0.1%, and a test has 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity for both, can the same positive test result mean very different things?"
    shortAnswer: "Yes. The rarer the disease, the lower the probability that a positive test is correct, even with the same test accuracy. This is the most-cited misuse of medical statistics and a perfect Bayes-rule example."
    detailed: |
      Let's compute. For the 1% base rate disease: out of 10,000 people, 100 have the disease, 9,900 don't. The test catches 99 of the 100 sick (sensitivity 99%). Of the 9,900 healthy, it falsely flags 99 (specificity 99% → 1% false positive). So 99 + 99 = 198 positives, of which 99 are real. P(disease | positive) = 99/198 = 50%.

      For the 0.1% base rate disease: out of 10,000, 10 have it, 9,990 don't. Test catches 9.9 of 10. False positives: ~99.9. So ~110 positives, ~10 real. P(disease | positive) ≈ 9%.

      Same test, same accuracy, very different meaning of a positive result. ML systems get this wrong constantly — a fraud classifier with 99% accuracy can be wildly useless if fraud is 0.1% of traffic.
    pitfalls:
      - "Class imbalance is the version of this problem ML practitioners hit most. We'll come back to it in A0.8 (evaluation metrics)."
  - question: "When a classifier outputs '0.92', what does that number mean?"
    shortAnswer: "Ideally, it's the model's estimate of P(label = positive | features). If well-calibrated, 0.92 means 'if I made many predictions like this, about 92% of them would be positive'. Most models are not perfectly calibrated."
    detailed: |
      Calibration is a separate question from accuracy. A model can be 90% accurate while being badly calibrated — e.g., outputting 0.99 confidence on examples that are actually right only 70% of the time. Neural networks trained with cross-entropy tend to be overconfident.

      Calibration is fixed post-hoc with techniques like temperature scaling or Platt scaling — applied after training, before deployment, on the validation set. For decisions where probability matters (e.g., 'should I send this user to a human reviewer?'), calibration matters as much as accuracy.
    pitfalls:
      - "Softmax outputs look like probabilities and are sometimes used like probabilities — but raw, uncalibrated softmax outputs are not reliable probability estimates."

  A *random variable* is a quantity whose value depends on chance. A *probability distribution*
  describes how likely each value is. *Conditional probability* `P(A|B)` is the probability of A
  given that B is true. *Bayes' rule* is the equation that flips a conditional probability around —
  and it's the foundation under most probabilistic ML. None of this requires you to like dice or
  playing cards; we'll use spam.
</TldrCallout>

## Probability is a language for talking about uncertainty

Machine learning models almost never produce a definite "this is X". They
produce a _probability_ — a number between 0 and 1 expressing how confident
the model is. To read those numbers (and to debug them when they're wrong),
you need a small amount of probability vocabulary. We're going to keep it
small.

## Random variables and distributions

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Random Variable"
  definition="A quantity whose value depends on the outcome of a random process. Coin flips, dice rolls, heights of strangers, whether a customer will churn — all are random variables. In ML, observations, predictions, and even parameters are typically modeled as random variables."
>
  A random variable
</GlossaryTerm>
is something whose value depends on chance. The outcome of a coin flip is a random variable with two
possible values. The number on a die is a random variable with six. The cost of a stranger's monthly
rent is a random variable that can take many values.

<GlossaryTerm term="Probability Distribution" definition="A description of how a random variable's values are spread out — which are likely, which are rare. For discrete variables, a table of probabilities per value. For continuous variables, a density function. Either way, the probabilities sum or integrate to exactly 1 over all possible outcomes.">The distribution</GlossaryTerm> of a random variable tells you which values are likely.
For a fair coin, the distribution is `{heads: 0.5, tails: 0.5}`. For a fair
die, each of the six faces has probability `1/6`. For continuous quantities
like rent, you can't list probabilities for every possible value (there are
infinitely many) — so you describe a *density function* instead. The famous
bell curve is a particular kind of density: the *Gaussian*.

The defining property of any probability distribution: the probabilities sum
to exactly 1 across all possible outcomes. If they don't, it isn't a
distribution.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Think of probability like budgeting your beliefs. You have a fixed budget of 1.0 worth of belief,
  and you have to distribute it among the possible outcomes. Spend more on the ones you think are
  likely, less on the ones you think are rare. Whatever your beliefs, the budget always totals 1.
</AnalogyCallout>

## Conditional probability

Now we get to the most useful idea in this lesson.

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Conditional Probability"
  definition="The probability of one event given that another event has occurred. Written P(A|B), read 'probability of A given B'. Often very different from P(A) — observing B can dramatically change how likely A becomes."
>
  Conditional probability,
</GlossaryTerm>
written `P(A | B)` and read "probability of A given B", is the probability of A *given that B
happened*. It's how you express "updates to beliefs after seeing evidence".

Example. Suppose 1% of emails in your inbox are spam. Just sitting there,
`P(spam) = 0.01`. But suppose you know an email contains the word "lottery".
Of all emails with "lottery", maybe 60% are spam. So `P(spam | 'lottery') =
0.6`. The unconditional probability is 1%; the conditional probability,
given the word "lottery" is present, is 60%.

Every classifier you'll ever build is, at heart, computing conditional
probabilities of the form `P(label | features)`.

## Bayes' rule

The most important formula in this lesson — and arguably in ML — is{" "}

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Bayes' Rule"
  definition="The formula P(A|B) = P(B|A) · P(A) / P(B). It connects the two directions of conditional probability and is the principled way to update a belief P(A) after observing evidence B. Underlies essentially all probabilistic ML, from naive Bayes to modern Bayesian deep learning."
>
  Bayes' rule
</GlossaryTerm>
:

```
P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)
```

It says: the probability of A given B equals the probability of B given A,
times the prior probability of A, divided by the probability of B. In
words: if you know how often B happens when A is true (the _likelihood_),
and how often A happens at all (the _prior_), you can compute how often A
is true when you see B (the _posterior_).

<WorkedExample title="Bayes' rule applied to a spam filter">

We're building a spam filter. Suppose we know three things from our data:

- `P(spam) = 0.20`. 20% of all incoming email is spam.
- `P('lottery' | spam) = 0.30`. 30% of spam contains the word "lottery".
- `P('lottery' | not spam) = 0.001`. Only 0.1% of legitimate email mentions
  lottery.

An email arrives containing "lottery". What's the probability it's spam?

Apply Bayes' rule. The numerator is `P('lottery' | spam) · P(spam) = 0.30 ·
0.20 = 0.06`.

The denominator `P('lottery')` we have to compute by summing over both
possibilities:

```
P('lottery') = P('lottery'|spam)·P(spam) + P('lottery'|not spam)·P(not spam)
             = 0.30 · 0.20 + 0.001 · 0.80
             = 0.06 + 0.0008
             = 0.0608
```

Posterior:

```
P(spam | 'lottery') = 0.06 / 0.0608 ≈ 0.987
```

The word "lottery" pushed our belief from 20% (the prior) to about 99% (the
posterior). That's Bayes' rule doing its job: combining the evidence
(likelihood ratio of "lottery") with the base rate.

</WorkedExample>

## Play with Bayes' rule yourself

The number that surprises most people: how _low_ the posterior can stay
even with a very accurate test, when the prior is small. The calculator
below lets you feel it.

<BayesCalculator />

Set the prior to 0.01 (a 1% base rate). Keep the test at 99% / 99%
sensitivity and specificity. Look at the posterior — barely above 50%.
Now slide the prior up to 0.10. Watch the posterior leap past 90%. The
test didn't get better; the population did. **This is why diagnostic
labs always know their base rates.**

This is exactly the calculation a _naive Bayes_ spam classifier performs,
just with many words instead of one. It's a one-paragraph algorithm that
formed the backbone of email spam filtering for two decades.

## Why this matters in practice

Three places probability shows up daily for ML engineers:

1. **Calibrated outputs.** When a classifier outputs `0.92`, that should
   mean "92% probability". For a well-calibrated model, of all predictions
   labelled 0.92, about 92% are correct. Neural networks are often
   miscalibrated out of the box — they tend to be overconfident — and need
   post-hoc calibration before they're trustworthy for downstream decisions.

2. **The base-rate trap.** A classifier that's 99% accurate sounds great
   until you remember that if the positive class is 1% of traffic, predicting
   "negative" always gets you 99% accuracy and zero usefulness. The same
   trap shows up in medical tests, fraud detection, and rare-event
   prediction. Bayes' rule is the way out.

3. **Loss functions are likelihoods.** Cross-entropy — the loss function
   used to train almost every modern classifier — is literally the negative
   log of a likelihood. Training a network with cross-entropy is _maximum
   likelihood estimation_ in disguise. Once you see this, large chunks of
   ML stop being mysterious.
