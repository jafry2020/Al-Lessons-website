  - question: "What is a vector, in ML terms?"
    shortAnswer: "An ordered list of numbers. In ML, a vector typically represents either one sample (each number is a feature value) or one feature across samples. The same vector can be thought of as a point in space or as a direction."
    detailed: |
      A vector with 3 components like (1.5, 3, 2010) can stand for one row of data — say, a house's (sqft-in-thousands, bedrooms, year-built). It's also a point in three-dimensional space, three steps along each axis. The same triple can be thought of as a *direction* from the origin to that point. ML uses all three interpretations interchangeably depending on context.

      Modern deep learning routinely uses vectors with thousands of components. A word embedding might be a 768-dimensional vector — a point in 768-dimensional space. We can't visualize that directly; we use 2D analogies and trust the math.
    pitfalls:
      - "Vectors in ML always have a basis — each component means something specific. A vector of features isn't interchangeable with a vector of pixel intensities even if they're both 'just vectors'."
  - question: "What is a dot product, and why is it the most-used operation in all of ML?"
    shortAnswer: "Multiply corresponding elements of two vectors, then sum. It measures alignment: high when the vectors point in similar directions, zero when perpendicular, negative when opposed. It's the building block of nearly every model — linear regression, neural networks, attention."
    detailed: |
      For vectors `a = (a1, a2, a3)` and `b = (b1, b2, b3)`, the dot product is `a·b = a1·b1 + a2·b2 + a3·b3`. Geometrically, `a·b = |a|·|b|·cos(θ)`, where θ is the angle between them.

      That second form is the key to its meaning. When two vectors point in the same direction, cos(θ) = 1 and the dot product is maximized. When they're perpendicular, cos(θ) = 0. When they point opposite, cos(θ) = -1.

      ML uses this to measure similarity: how similar are two word embeddings, two user-preference vectors, two image features. Nearly every score, similarity, and attention-weight in modern ML is a dot product (often normalized).
    pitfalls:
      - "Dot product doesn't normalize for magnitude — a high dot product can mean alignment OR just very long vectors. Cosine similarity = dot product divided by magnitudes — fixes this."
  - question: "What does a matrix do to a vector?"
    shortAnswer: "It transforms the vector — rotates it, scales it, projects it, or some combination. Geometrically, multiplying a matrix by a vector says 'apply this transformation to that point'."
    detailed: |
      Matrix-vector multiplication `Mv` produces a new vector that's `v` reshaped by the transformation `M`. A diagonal matrix scales along each axis (stretches some, shrinks others). A rotation matrix rotates `v` around the origin. A projection matrix flattens `v` onto a lower-dimensional subspace.

      In a neural network, each layer is a matrix multiplication (plus a bias and a non-linearity). The matrix encodes 'how does this layer transform its input'. Training updates the matrix.
    pitfalls:
      - "Matrix multiplication is not commutative: `Mv` makes sense, `vM` requires `v` to be a row vector with compatible shape. Confusing the two is a common bug for people coming from a math background expecting commutativity."
  - question: "Why do dimensions of `M` and `v` have to match?"
    shortAnswer: "Each row of M is dotted with v to produce one element of the output. So the number of columns of M must equal the number of components of v. Otherwise the dot products don't make sense."
    detailed: |
      If M is a 4×3 matrix and v is a 3-component vector, the result Mv is a 4-component vector. Each of the 4 output components comes from dotting one row of M (3 numbers) with v (3 numbers).

      If you try to multiply a 4×3 matrix by a 5-component vector, the dot products are undefined — too many components. Shape mismatches are the most common runtime error in deep learning code; tensor libraries make this visible by failing loudly.
    pitfalls:
      - "Shape errors in NumPy/PyTorch are not subtle bugs — they raise. Read the error and check your shapes; don't try to outsmart the library."
  - question: "What is a matrix multiplication geometrically?"
    shortAnswer: "Composition of two transformations. `(M·N)v` is 'first apply N to v, then apply M to the result'. The product matrix `M·N` encodes the combined effect."
    detailed: |
      If N rotates by 30 degrees and M scales by 2, then M·N is the matrix that 'rotates by 30 then scales by 2'. The order matters: N·M scales first, then rotates — a different transformation in general.

      In a neural network, stacking layers is matrix composition. Layer 2's matrix times layer 1's matrix gives the composite transformation from input to layer-2 output. (Plus non-linearities, which prevent the stack from collapsing into a single matrix — that's the whole point of activation functions.)
    pitfalls:
      - "Matrix product order matters: AB ≠ BA in general."
      - "Stacking purely linear layers without non-linearities is mathematically equivalent to one linear layer. Activation functions are what give deep networks their expressive power (A4.3 covers this)."
  - question: "What's a high-dimensional space, and why does intuition break there?"
    shortAnswer: "A space with many independent directions — modern ML uses spaces with hundreds or thousands. 2D and 3D intuition often misleads here. Random points are typically far from each other and roughly equidistant — the 'curse of dimensionality'."
    detailed: |
      In 2D, a random pair of points has variable distance — some are close, some far. In 1000-dimensional space, all randomly-sampled pairs end up with approximately the same distance from each other. Nearest-neighbor methods get less useful as dimensions grow because 'nearest' loses its meaning.

      Other counter-intuitive facts about high-dimensional space: most of the volume of a high-d ball sits near its surface; random vectors are almost-always almost-perpendicular; the difference between the closest point and the farthest point shrinks relative to their distances.

      Models like deep neural nets work *despite* these facts because they don't treat the high-dimensional space as a flat geometry — they learn to project onto useful lower-dimensional subspaces.
    pitfalls:
      - "Don't picture 1000-dimensional space; it'll lie to you. Trust the math."
      - "The curse of dimensionality is exactly why dimensionality-reduction methods (PCA, UMAP, t-SNE — covered in module A3) exist."
  - question: "When ML papers talk about an 'embedding', what does that mean in linear-algebra terms?"
    shortAnswer: "A learned vector representation. An embedding maps a discrete object (a word, a user, an image) into a fixed-dimensional vector space so similar objects land near each other. The vector *is* the embedding."
    detailed: |
      Take the word 'king'. To do math on it, you need a numerical representation. An embedding model maps 'king' to a vector like (0.12, -0.34, 0.88, ...) in, say, 768-dimensional space. The mapping is learned: words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors. The famous toy example: vector(king) - vector(man) + vector(woman) ≈ vector(queen). That works because the embedding has captured something about gender as a direction in the space.

      All of modern NLP, recommender systems, and search are built on embeddings.
    pitfalls:
      - "Embedding dimensions don't have human-interpretable names. The 5th dimension isn't 'gender' — gender lives somewhere in a learned linear combination of many dimensions."

<TldrCallout>
  A *vector* is a list of numbers. In ML, one vector per sample (each number is a feature value), or
  one vector per feature (across samples). A *dot product* is the multiply-then-sum operation
  between two vectors; it measures how aligned they are and is the building block of nearly every
  model. A *matrix* is a stack of vectors; multiplying a matrix by a vector transforms it — rotates,
  scales, projects. That's the whole story we'll need for the rest of this course.
</TldrCallout>

## Vectors

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Vector"
  definition="An ordered list of numbers. In ML, a vector typically represents one sample (each number is a feature value) or one feature across samples. The same vector can be thought of as a point in space or as a direction from the origin."
>
  A vector
</GlossaryTerm>
is just a list of numbers, but it has two interpretations that you'll switch between constantly.

**As a point in space.** A 2D vector `(3, 4)` is the point three units right
and four units up. A 3D vector `(3, 4, 5)` adds a third axis. In ML, a 100-
dimensional vector is a point in 100-dimensional space — we can't picture
it, but the math is the same.

**As a direction.** The same `(3, 4)` is also a _direction_ — pointing from
the origin to that point. Length of that direction is `√(3² + 4²) = 5`. This
view is useful when we're measuring angles between vectors.

In ML, a vector usually represents one sample. For our house dataset, the
sample `(1450, 3, 02139, 1972)` is a 4-vector — one point in 4-dimensional
"house-feature space". A trained model is essentially a procedure for
slicing that space into regions and assigning a prediction to each region.

## The dot product

This is the single most important operation in the lesson.

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Dot Product"
  definition="An operation between two vectors that multiplies corresponding elements and sums them. Produces a scalar (a single number). Geometrically, it equals |a|·|b|·cos(θ), so it measures how aligned the two vectors are. The building block of nearly every operation in modern ML."
>
  The dot product
</GlossaryTerm>
of `a = (a₁, a₂, a₃)` and `b = (b₁, b₂, b₃)` is:

```
a · b = a₁·b₁ + a₂·b₂ + a₃·b₃
```

Multiply corresponding entries; sum. The result is a single number (a
scalar), not a vector.

Geometrically: `a · b = |a| · |b| · cos(θ)`, where `θ` is the angle between
the two vectors and `|a|, |b|` are their lengths.

That equation contains the soul of why dot products matter: **cos(θ)
measures how aligned the vectors are**. When they point the same way,
cos(0) = 1 and the dot product is maximized for the given lengths. When
they're perpendicular, cos(90°) = 0 and the dot product is zero. When they
point opposite, cos(180°) = -1 and the dot product is negative.

<WorkedExample title="A dot product, by hand">

Two house feature vectors, in `(sqft, bedrooms)`:

`a = (1500, 3)` (a 3-bedroom 1500 sqft house)
`b = (1450, 3)` (a 3-bedroom 1450 sqft house)
`c = (500, 1)` (a 1-bedroom 500 sqft house)

`a · b = 1500·1450 + 3·3 = 2,175,009` — large, because `a` and `b` are very
similar.

`a · c = 1500·500 + 3·1 = 750,003` — smaller, because `a` and `c` point
in similar-ish directions but `c` is much shorter.

To strip out the magnitude effect, divide by the lengths:

```
cos(θ_ab) = a·b / (|a|·|b|) ≈ 1.0  (essentially identical direction)
cos(θ_ac) = a·c / (|a|·|c|) ≈ 1.0  (also very aligned!)
```

Both pairs point in nearly the same direction in feature space, despite very
different magnitudes. This is _cosine similarity_ — the normalized dot
product. It's what every modern search and recommendation system uses to
measure "is item A like item B".

</WorkedExample>

A _linear regression_ model is literally a dot product:

```
prediction = w · x + b
```

where `w` is a vector of weights (parameters) and `x` is a vector of feature
values. The model computes the dot product of features with weights, adds a
bias, and that's the prediction. Most of the rest of ML stacks this idea —
many dot products in parallel, then a non-linearity, then more dot
products.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Imagine two arrows pointing out of the same spot. Their *dot product* measures how parallel they
  are, scaled by their lengths. Two arrows in the same direction: big positive dot product. Two
  perpendicular arrows: zero. Two opposite arrows: big negative. It's a single number that
  summarizes "how aligned are these two things?"
</AnalogyCallout>

## Matrices

A{" "}

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Matrix"
  definition="A two-dimensional array of numbers — rows and columns. Can be thought of as a stack of row vectors (each row is one sample) or a stack of column vectors (each column is one feature across samples). Multiplying a matrix by a vector transforms the vector geometrically."
>
  matrix
</GlossaryTerm>
is a rectangle of numbers — rows and columns. You can think of it as a stack of vectors. In ML, a
dataset of `N` samples each with `D` features is naturally a matrix with shape `N × D`: N rows, D
columns.

When you multiply a matrix by a vector, you get a new vector. Geometrically,
the matrix is _transforming_ the vector — rotating it, scaling it,
projecting it, or some combination.

A neural-network layer is, mathematically, a matrix multiplication followed
by adding a bias and applying a non-linearity. The matrix is the layer's
learnable weights; multiplying by it transforms the input vector into an
output vector that the next layer consumes.

```
output_layer1 = ReLU(W₁ · input + b₁)
output_layer2 = ReLU(W₂ · output_layer1 + b₂)
...
prediction = W_n · output_layer_{n-1} + b_n
```

That's the entire forward pass of a vanilla feedforward network, in two
lines of pseudo-math. Every layer: matrix multiplication, bias, non-
linearity. The non-linearity (ReLU, GELU, etc.) is what prevents the stack
of matrix multiplications from collapsing back into a single matrix — it's
the source of the network's expressive power.

## A note on high-dimensional spaces

ML routinely lives in spaces with hundreds or thousands of dimensions. Your
2D and 3D geometric intuition is going to lie to you there. Specifically:

- In 1000-dimensional space, two random vectors are _almost always almost
  perpendicular_. There is just a lot of room for them to differ.
- Distances between random points in high-dimensional space are almost all
  the same. The 'nearest' point and the 'farthest' point are barely
  distinguishable in relative terms.
- Most of the volume of a high-dimensional ball is concentrated near its
  surface, not its interior.

This is collectively known as the _curse of dimensionality_. It's why
nearest-neighbor methods degrade in high dimensions, and why we need
techniques like PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE (Module A3) to project down to spaces
we can reason about. Modern deep learning sidesteps the curse by learning
its own low-dimensional representations — embeddings — inside the high-
dimensional space.
