  - question: "What is the bias–variance trade-off?"
    shortAnswer: "The classical idea that as you make a model more complex, its bias (systematic error) decreases and its variance (sensitivity to the specific training set) increases. The total error has a U-shape; the goal is to find the bottom."
    detailed: |
      Think of bias as 'how wrong this model class is no matter how it's trained' and variance as 'how much the model's predictions wobble if you retrain it on a different random sample of training data'.

      A degree-1 polynomial fit to a curvy function has high bias (it's a line; it can't curve) and low variance (different training sets all yield similar lines). A degree-20 polynomial has low bias (it can fit anything) and high variance (different training samples wiggle wildly).

  - question: "What is regularization?"
    shortAnswer: "Anything that biases the training procedure toward simpler models. L1/L2 weight penalties, dropout, early stopping, data augmentation — all are regularization. The goal is to reduce variance at a small cost to bias."
    detailed: |
      Concretely: L2 regularization adds a penalty proportional to the sum of squared parameters. The optimizer is then minimizing 'loss + λ·(sum of squared weights)' instead of just loss. The penalty discourages very large weights, which corresponds to a simpler model.

      Dropout randomly disables some neurons each training step, preventing co-adaptation. Data augmentation creates synthetic training samples (rotations of images, paraphrases of text) to make the model robust to variations that don't matter. All of these reduce overfitting.
    pitfalls:
      - "Regularization isn't free — it adds bias to reduce variance. The trick is to apply just enough."
  - question: "What is early stopping, and why is it the simplest regularizer?"
    shortAnswer: "Stop training when validation loss stops improving. It's free (no extra computation), works for any model, and you don't have to pick a regularization strength — the validation curve tells you when to stop."
    detailed: |
      Conceptually: train for too long and the model starts memorizing instead of generalizing. The validation loss reflects this — it's a leading indicator that the model is leaving the 'generalization sweet spot'.

      Practical recipe: every N steps, evaluate on the validation set. Keep a running best validation loss. If validation loss hasn't improved in M evaluations, stop training and restore the parameters from the best evaluation. Tunable values: how often you evaluate, how patient you are.

      Almost every modern training loop uses early stopping. It composes well with other regularizers.
    pitfalls:
      - "If the validation set is too small, the loss curve is noisy and you'll stop too early or too late. Calibrate patience accordingly."
  - question: "Why does adding more training data reduce overfitting?"
    shortAnswer: "More data makes the noise harder to memorize. The model has to find patterns that hold across more examples, which forces it toward generalizable structure."
    detailed: |
      A model with 1 million parameters trained on 1000 samples can essentially memorize every sample — there's enough capacity to fit each one individually. The same model trained on 10 million samples can't memorize them all; it has to find compact representations that explain many samples at once. Those compact representations tend to generalize.

      The corollary: if a model is overfitting and you can get more data, do that before trying clever regularization tricks. More data usually beats more cleverness.
    pitfalls:
      - "More data only helps if it's drawn from the same distribution as your test data. More out-of-distribution data can hurt."
  - question: "What's 'double descent', and why is it confusing?"
    shortAnswer: "An observation that for very large modern neural networks, the classical U-curve of error-vs-model-size has a second descent past the apparent peak. The model can keep getting better as you keep making it bigger — sometimes way past the point classical theory predicts disaster."
    detailed: |
      In classical statistics: as model complexity grows, validation error first goes down (less bias), then goes back up (more variance) — the famous U. For modern deep learning, this is correct *up to a point*: validation error climbs, peaks somewhere around the parameter count equalling the dataset size, and then — surprisingly — descends again.

      Why this happens is still being argued. One reading: with extreme overparameterization, gradient descent prefers smooth, simple interpolating functions (implicit regularization), which generalize even though the model technically has the capacity to memorize.

      This is why you sometimes hear 'just make it bigger' as serious advice — for some architectures, on some data, more parameters keeps helping past where classical theory says they shouldn't.
    pitfalls:
      - "Double descent is real and observed, but it isn't a license to scale model size blindly. Compute, data, and inference cost still matter."

<TldrCallout>
  *Overfitting* is when a model has fit the training data so closely it fails on new data.
  *Underfitting* is when the model is too simple to fit the data at all. Between them is a sweet
  spot called the *bias–variance trade-off*. Pushing a model toward that sweet spot is what
  *regularization* does — penalizing complexity, dropping connections, stopping training early. The
  single best diagnostic is the gap between training loss and validation loss.
</TldrCallout>

## The story in one sentence

You can fit your training data perfectly. That is almost always bad news.

## What overfitting looks like

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Overfitting"
  definition="When a model has fit the training data so closely — including its noise and quirks — that it performs poorly on new data. Symptoms: low training loss, high validation loss, and a widening gap between the two as training continues."
>
  Overfitting
</GlossaryTerm>
is when a model has learned the training set so well it has learned its noise — and the noise
doesn't generalize. New data has *different* noise, so the model's predictions don't transfer.

The classic diagnostic plot:

```
Training loss:    ↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓↓
Validation loss:  ↓↓↓↓↓↓↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑
                        ^
                  best generalization is here
```

Training loss keeps going down (the model keeps memorizing). Validation
loss bottoms out, then _rises_ — that rise is the model getting worse on
new data even as it gets better on training data. The peak usefulness of
the model is at the bottom of the validation curve, not the training
curve. Stop training there.

## What underfitting looks like

The opposite problem.{" "}

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Underfitting"
  definition="When a model is too simple (or too constrained) to fit the underlying pattern in the data. Symptoms: high training loss and high validation loss, both unable to come down."
>
  Underfitting
</GlossaryTerm>
is when the model is too simple to capture the patterns in the data. Training loss stays high;
validation loss also stays high; neither is getting better.

A straight line trying to fit a U-shape underfits — no matter where you set
the slope and intercept, a line can't bend. The architecture is the
ceiling. To fix underfitting: more capacity, more features, different
architecture.

Underfitting is easier to fix than overfitting. The diagnostic question is
simple: can you reduce _training_ loss further by adding capacity? If yes,
your model is under-capacity, not over-capacity.

## The bias–variance trade-off

The classical framing of the underfit–overfit spectrum is the{" "}

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Bias-Variance Trade-off"
  definition="The classical statistical idea that model error decomposes into bias (systematic error from a too-simple model class) and variance (sensitivity to which specific training data the model saw). Making a model more complex reduces bias but increases variance, and total error has a U-shape with a sweet spot in the middle."
>
  bias–variance trade-off
</GlossaryTerm>
:

- **Bias** is systematic error. A model class that is too simple to capture
  the true pattern has high bias — no matter how you train it, it can't get
  close. A line trying to fit a curve has high bias.
- **Variance** is sensitivity to the specific training data. A flexible
  model — say, a degree-20 polynomial — fits any wiggle in any training
  set. Train it on two different random samples and you get very different
  functions. That sensitivity is variance.

As you increase model complexity, bias goes down and variance goes up. Total
error (bias + variance + irreducible noise) has a U-shape; somewhere in the
middle is the best balance. Finding that balance is the central craft of
applied ML.

<WorkedExample title="Polynomial fitting demonstrates the U-curve">

Imagine the true relationship between `x` and `y` is `y = sin(x) +
noise`. We don't know that; we just see 30 noisy points.

Fit a degree-1 polynomial (a line). It can't curve, so it captures the
overall trend but misses the sine wave. _Training loss is high, validation
loss is high._ High bias, low variance. Underfit.

Fit a degree-5 polynomial. It can capture one or two cycles of the sine
wave. Training loss is moderate; validation loss is moderate but improved.
Closer to balanced.

Fit a degree-20 polynomial. With enough flexibility, it threads precisely
through the training points — every single one. Training loss is nearly
zero. But the wiggle between training points is wild, and the model
predicts garbage on validation points. _Training loss tiny, validation
loss huge._ Low bias, high variance. Overfit.

Plot validation loss against polynomial degree: the U-curve. The bottom
sits around degree 4–6 — enough flexibility to capture the sine wave, not
enough to chase the noise.

</WorkedExample>

<AnalogyCallout>
  Imagine studying for an exam. *Underfitting* is reading the textbook once, getting the gist,
  missing details — you'll get the big questions right and the specific ones wrong. *Overfitting* is
  memorizing every example problem verbatim — you'll ace anything identical to what you've seen and
  fail anything new. The sweet spot is understanding the underlying principles deeply enough to
  solve novel problems. That's what generalization means.
</AnalogyCallout>

## Regularization: nudging away from overfitting

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Regularization"
  definition="Any modification to training that biases the model toward simpler solutions. Examples include L1/L2 weight penalties, dropout, data augmentation, and early stopping. The goal is to reduce variance at the cost of a small amount of bias — moving down the right side of the U-curve."
>
  Regularization
</GlossaryTerm>
is the family of techniques that bias the training procedure toward simpler models. Several flavors,
all useful in practice:

- **L2 (weight decay)** adds a penalty to the loss for large parameter
  values. The optimizer trades a little training fit for smaller weights.
  Cheap, ubiquitous, the default for most modern models.
- **L1** penalizes the absolute value of weights instead of the square.
  Same idea, but more aggressive at pushing weights to exactly zero — used
  for feature selection.
- **Dropout** randomly disables a fraction of neurons during each training
  step. Forces the network to spread information across many units rather
  than relying on a few. Specific to neural nets.
- **Data augmentation** synthesizes new training examples (rotations of
  images, paraphrases of sentences). More data is the single best
  regularizer; augmentation is how you fake having more.
- **Early stopping** simply quits when validation loss stops improving.
  Free; always available; works for any model.

The right amount of regularization depends on the model and data. Too
little: still overfits. Too much: under-fits. Tune on the validation set
(never the test set).

## Why zero training loss is suspicious

If a model achieves zero training loss, one of three things is true:

1. The dataset is trivially easy (every input has a unique unambiguous
   answer that the model can copy).
2. The model is enormously over-capacity for the dataset and has memorized
   everything.
3. There's a bug — possibly data leakage (A0.3) — that lets the model see
   the answer.

For real-world problems with real-world noise, the truth is rarely
deterministic. Some labels are wrong, some inputs are ambiguous. A model
that achieves zero training loss has fit through the noise as well as the
signal, which means part of what it learned is wrong. Validation loss will
expose this.

## A note on modern deep learning

The classical bias–variance trade-off was developed before deep learning,
and modern deep networks sometimes violate the textbook story. Models with
hundreds of billions of parameters trained on enormous datasets can have
low bias _and_ low variance simultaneously — a phenomenon related to
'double descent'. The classical U-curve is still a useful frame for the
small- to medium-data regime that most engineers actually work in. Just
know it isn't a law of physics.
