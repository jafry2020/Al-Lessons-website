  - question: "In one sentence: what is a machine-learning model?"
    shortAnswer: "A parameterized function — it takes an input, runs it through some math involving the parameters, and produces a prediction. Training is the process of choosing those parameter values."
    detailed: |
      A model has two parts. The *architecture* is the shape of the function — for example, 'a straight line through two-dimensional space' or 'a five-layer neural network with these dimensions'. The *parameters* are the specific numbers inside that shape — the slope and intercept of the line, or the millions of weights inside the neural network.

      Before training, the parameters are random. After training, they encode whatever pattern the model managed to extract from the data. The architecture is something a human picks; the parameters are something the training procedure picks.
    pitfalls:
      - "'Model' can mean either the architecture, the trained artifact, or the whole codebase that produces predictions. Context disambiguates — usually."
      - "Two models with the same architecture and the same training data can end up with different parameters and slightly different predictions. Training is non-deterministic in practice."
  - question: "What is a loss function, and why is it the centerpiece of training?"
    shortAnswer: "A loss function takes a prediction and the true answer and produces a single number measuring how wrong the prediction is. Training searches for parameters that make the average loss across the training data as small as possible."
    detailed: |
      Without a loss, there is no notion of 'better' or 'worse' parameters — and therefore no way to improve. Common losses include squared error (for regression, where outputs are continuous) and cross-entropy (for classification, where outputs are probabilities). The choice of loss function shapes what the model optimizes for: a model trained with squared error punishes outliers harder than one trained with mean absolute error, and that changes which patterns it locks onto.

      The loss is averaged across all training samples; training tries to minimize that average. A model that scores low loss on the training data isn't necessarily good — that's what generalization measures (A0.7).
    pitfalls:
      - "Picking the wrong loss for the task is one of the most common subtle bugs. Classifying with squared error sort of works but is strictly worse than cross-entropy."
      - "Loss values are not directly comparable across tasks or even across different loss functions — '0.04 loss' means nothing in isolation."
  - question: "What's the difference between training and inference?"
    shortAnswer: "Training is the parameter-search phase: expensive, batched, infrequent. Inference is the use phase: cheap per call, real-time, frequent. A trained model is a frozen function — inference just runs it."
    detailed: |
      During training, the model sees data, makes predictions, computes loss, and updates parameters in tiny increments. This typically requires GPUs, lots of memory, and hours-to-weeks of compute.

      During inference, the parameters are frozen. The model takes an input and computes the prediction in one forward pass. No gradients, no parameter updates. Inference is what serves user traffic and what the product economics live on. Modern deep models are designed so inference is hundreds to thousands of times cheaper than the training step that produced them.
    pitfalls:
      - "Engineers often confuse training-step latency with inference latency when reading papers — they're orders of magnitude apart."
      - "Total inference cost over the model's lifetime often exceeds total training cost for popular products."
  - question: "If the model is just a function with parameters, why are some models 'better' than others on the same data?"
    shortAnswer: "Two reasons: (a) different architectures have different function shapes — some can express patterns others can't; (b) different training procedures (loss, optimizer, regularization) find different parameter values inside the same architecture."
    detailed: |
      A straight line cannot fit a U-shaped pattern, no matter what slope and intercept you pick. The architecture is the *expressiveness ceiling*. A deep neural network can approximate almost any function — but it has to actually find good parameters, and that's where the training procedure matters. Better optimizers, better initial values, better regularization all matter.

      The art of applied ML is choosing an architecture whose ceiling exceeds your problem's demands, then training it well enough to get close to that ceiling.
    pitfalls:
      - "Picking a bigger model when training is the bottleneck won't help. Picking a smaller model when expressiveness is the bottleneck won't either. Diagnose first."
  - question: "What are parameters versus hyperparameters?"
    shortAnswer: "Parameters are values the model learns during training. Hyperparameters are values the engineer sets before training, like learning rate, model size, or which optimizer to use."
    detailed: |
      Both are knobs that affect performance, but they sit at different levels. Parameters live inside the model and are updated by the training algorithm. Hyperparameters live outside the model and shape how training happens.

      In a linear regression `y = w · x + b`, `w` and `b` are parameters. The learning rate (how fast `w` and `b` are nudged each step) is a hyperparameter. Hyperparameters are usually tuned by trying a few combinations on a validation set; parameters are tuned by gradient descent on the training set.
    pitfalls:
      - "Tuning hyperparameters on the test set is data leakage in slow motion. Use a separate validation split."
  - question: "What does it mean for a model to 'overfit' the training data?"
    shortAnswer: "Overfitting is when the model memorizes the training data — including its noise — so well that it does worse on new data. Training loss goes down; test loss goes up."
    detailed: |
      A model that memorizes can look perfect on the training set. The catch is that real-world data is noisy, and some of what the model 'learned' is just the noise. New data has different noise, so the memorized patterns don't transfer.

      The fix is to limit how flexibly the model can fit the data: smaller models, regularization, dropout, early stopping, more training data. We have a full lesson on this (A0.7); for now, the gut-feel is: if your training loss is way lower than your validation loss, you're memorizing.
    pitfalls:
      - "Overfitting is not the same as 'high training loss'. A high-bias model that underfits has high training loss too. Different problem, different fix."
  - question: "If training takes hours or weeks, how do engineers iterate?"
    shortAnswer: "Smaller experiments. Tiny model on a subset of data first; only scale up after the recipe works. The training run that produces the final model is usually preceded by dozens of small probes."
    detailed: |
      The cost of a full training run kills your ability to learn from mistakes. The standard practice is to debug on toy versions: smaller dataset, smaller model, faster optimizer settings. Once the pipeline works end-to-end at toy scale, you scale up. This is the 'fast feedback loop' that separates productive ML teams from frustrated ones.

      Concretely: if your full training run takes 24 hours and fails 30 minutes in because of a data bug, you've wasted half a day. The same bug in a 2-minute toy run is a minor annoyance.
    pitfalls:
      - "Premature scaling — moving to full data before the recipe works — is the most common form of wasted compute in industry."

<TldrCallout>
  A machine-learning *model* is a function with adjustable knobs called *parameters*. *Training* is
  the procedure that adjusts those knobs by comparing the model's predictions to the right answers,
  measuring the gap with a *loss function*, and nudging the parameters in whichever direction
  shrinks the gap. *Inference* is what happens once training is done — the knobs freeze and the
  function gets used.
</TldrCallout>

## The four words you need

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Model"
  definition="A function with learnable parameters that maps inputs to predictions. The architecture is the shape of the function; the parameters are the specific numbers inside it. Before training, the parameters are random; after training, they encode whatever pattern the model extracted from the data."
>
  Model.
</GlossaryTerm>
A model is a function. It takes an input and produces a prediction. The same shape of function can
produce different predictions depending on its parameters.

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Parameter"
  definition="A number inside the model that is adjusted during training. Linear regression has two parameters (slope and intercept); a modern large language model has hundreds of billions. Parameters encode what the model has learned from data."
>
  Parameter.
</GlossaryTerm>
The numbers that move during training. A line `y = w·x + b` has two parameters: `w` and `b`.

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Loss Function"
  definition="A function that takes a prediction and a true answer and returns a number measuring how wrong the prediction is. Training searches for parameter values that minimize the average loss across the training data. Common choices include squared error (for regression) and cross-entropy (for classification)."
>
  Loss function.
</GlossaryTerm>
A measurement of how wrong the model is. Low loss means the predictions are close to the right
answers; high loss means they aren't.

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Training"
  definition="The procedure that searches for parameter values which minimize the loss on the training data. Typically iterative: make predictions, measure loss, nudge parameters, repeat."
>
  Training.
</GlossaryTerm>
The procedure that adjusts parameters to make loss go down. Inference is the opposite — using the
frozen, trained model on new inputs.

## A model is a function — that's it

The biggest mental block beginners have is treating a model as something
mysterious. It isn't. It's a function `f(x)` that takes an input `x` and
returns a prediction `y`. The function has knobs — the parameters — and
their values determine what `f` actually computes.

A linear regression is the simplest example: `f(x) = w · x + b`. Two
parameters, `w` and `b`. Pick `w = 2` and `b = 1`, and `f(3) = 7`. Pick `w =
0` and `b = 0`, and `f(3) = 0`. Same shape of function, different
predictions, because the parameters differ.

A deep neural network is the same idea — just a much more complicated
function, often with hundreds of millions of parameters. The principle is
unchanged: the function has knobs, training adjusts them, inference uses the
adjusted version.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Imagine an old-school radio with a tuning dial. The radio is the architecture. The dial position
  is the parameter. Static is bad predictions; the clear station is good predictions. Training is
  the process of slowly turning the dial until the static fades. Inference is what happens once the
  station is dialed in: you just listen.
</AnalogyCallout>

## What does "training" actually do?

Training is parameter search. Specifically, it's the search for parameter
values that make the model wrong as little as possible across the training
data. Three pieces:

1. **A way to measure wrongness.** This is the loss function. Given a
   prediction and the right answer, it returns a number. Higher number = more
   wrong.
2. **A way to know which direction to nudge each parameter.** This is the
   gradient (we'll cover the math in A0.6). The gradient tells us, for each
   parameter, which way it should move to reduce loss.
3. **A way to actually do the nudging.** This is the optimizer (A4.5 has the
   gory details for neural nets).

Repeat steps 1–3 over and over, on small batches of data, until the loss
stops going down or you run out of patience.

<WorkedExample title="Training a one-parameter model by hand">

Suppose we want a model that predicts house prices from square footage. We
pick the simplest possible architecture: `price = w · sqft`. One parameter,
`w`. (Real models would also have an intercept and more features; we're
stripping it down.)

We have three training examples:

| sqft | price   |
| ---- | ------- |
| 1000 | 200,000 |
| 1500 | 320,000 |
| 2000 | 410,000 |

We pick our loss as squared error: `(prediction − true)²`, summed across the
three rows.

Start with `w = 100` (a random guess). Predictions are 100,000, 150,000,
200,000. The squared errors are roughly 10 billion each — a total loss
around 30 billion. Way off.

Try `w = 200`. Predictions are 200,000, 300,000, 400,000. The errors are
much smaller — total loss around 500 million. Better.

Try `w = 210`. Predictions are 210,000, 315,000, 420,000. Loss is around 200
million. Better still.

Keep going. The training procedure does exactly this — but it doesn't try
random values, it follows the gradient downhill. After a few hundred steps,
`w` settles near the value that minimizes the loss across the three rows.
That settled value is the trained model.

This is exactly how every modern ML system works. Bigger models have more
parameters and need more sophisticated optimizers, but the conceptual
loop — predict, measure loss, nudge — is identical.

</WorkedExample>

## Training and inference are two different jobs

Once training is done, the parameters freeze. From then on, the model just
runs forward — input goes in, prediction comes out, no learning, no updates,
no loss computation. This is inference.

Inference is dramatically cheaper than training. Training a large model
might cost tens of millions of dollars in compute, take weeks, and require
thousands of GPUs. The same model, once trained, can run inference on a
single GPU in milliseconds.

The asymmetry shapes how products are built. You train once (or every few
weeks), but you serve inference traffic constantly. Most engineering effort
in production ML goes into the inference path: latency budgets, autoscaling,
caching, fallback behavior. Training is more of a research-style activity.

## What changes between simple models and deep learning

The story above — function with parameters, loss, training loop — is _the
same story_ for linear regression and for a 175-billion-parameter language
model. The difference is scale, expressiveness, and infrastructure:

- Linear regression has 2 parameters; a deep model has billions.
- Linear regression's architecture can only fit straight lines; deep
  models can approximate almost anything.
- Linear regression trains in a fraction of a second on a laptop; deep
  models take weeks across thousands of GPUs.

But the _recipe_ is unchanged. Every later lesson in this track is going to
re-use these four words — model, parameter, loss, training — over and over.
Get them solid here.
