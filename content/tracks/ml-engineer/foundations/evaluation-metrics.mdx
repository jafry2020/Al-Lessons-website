importantQuestions:
  - question: "What is accuracy, and when does it lie?"
    shortAnswer: "Accuracy is the fraction of predictions that are correct. It lies whenever the classes are imbalanced — a model that always predicts the majority class can score 99% accuracy and be useless."
    detailed: |
      For a dataset where 1% of samples are fraud, a model that says 'not fraud' to everything scores 99% accuracy. It catches zero fraud and is worse than a coin flip in the only way that matters. Yet reported accuracy makes it look like a winning model.

      Use accuracy only when the classes are roughly balanced and false positives and false negatives are roughly equally bad. For everything else — fraud, disease screening, anomaly detection, content moderation, churn prediction — accuracy is the wrong default.
    pitfalls:
      - "If someone reports 'high accuracy' without telling you the class balance, ask for the class balance before believing the number."
  - question: "What is precision?"
    shortAnswer: "Of the predictions the model labeled positive, what fraction are actually positive? Precision = true positives / (true positives + false positives). High precision means 'when the model says yes, it's right'."
    detailed: |
      Precision is about the *quality* of positive predictions. A spam filter with 95% precision means: of all the emails it flagged as spam, 95% really are spam (only 5% are legitimate emails wrongly flagged).

      When does precision matter most? When false positives are expensive. Examples: a fraud-detection system that freezes accounts (false positive = upset customer), a medical screening system whose positives go to expensive follow-up tests, a spam filter where filed legitimate emails are intolerable.
    pitfalls:
      - "100% precision can be trivially achieved by predicting positive only when the model is 99.9% confident — which means almost never. Always pair precision with recall."
  - question: "What is recall?"
    shortAnswer: "Of all actual positive samples, what fraction did the model find? Recall = true positives / (true positives + false negatives). High recall means 'the model catches most of the real positives'."
    detailed: |
      Recall is about the *coverage* of positive predictions. A disease screening test with 95% recall catches 95% of actual sick patients. The 5% it misses are false negatives — diseases that went undetected.

      When does recall matter most? When false negatives are expensive. Examples: cancer screening (missing a real case is catastrophic), security threat detection, search relevance (missing the right result is bad), search-and-rescue. In all of these, you'd rather get a few false alarms than miss a real positive.
    pitfalls:
      - "100% recall can be trivially achieved by predicting positive for everything. Always pair recall with precision."
  - question: "Why do precision and recall trade off?"
    shortAnswer: "Most classifiers output a probability and convert to a yes/no decision via a threshold. Lowering the threshold flags more samples as positive — recall goes up, precision goes down. Raising it does the opposite. You can pick a point on this curve; you can't have both maximized."
    detailed: |
      Imagine a fraud classifier that outputs a fraud probability between 0 and 1. The default threshold is 0.5: predict 'fraud' if probability ≥ 0.5. With threshold 0.3, the model labels more transactions as fraud — it catches more real fraud (higher recall) but also flags more legitimate transactions (lower precision). With threshold 0.7, the opposite.

      The whole *precision-recall curve* sweeps every possible threshold. The right operating point depends on the cost asymmetry: if false positives are cheaper than false negatives, set a low threshold; if the reverse, set a high one.
    pitfalls:
      - "Defaulting to threshold 0.5 because the math 'looks like the middle' is a habit, not a justified choice. Pick the threshold based on the business cost matrix."
  - question: "What is F1, and when do you use it?"
    shortAnswer: "The harmonic mean of precision and recall. F1 = 2 · P · R / (P + R). It's a single number that punishes models with very unbalanced precision and recall. Useful when you want one metric to summarize, but you should still look at P and R separately."
    detailed: |
      Why harmonic mean instead of arithmetic? Because harmonic mean is pulled down by the smaller of the two. A model with precision 1.0 and recall 0.0 has an arithmetic mean of 0.5 (sounds OK) but an F1 of 0 (correctly terrible). F1 only rewards models that are good at *both*.

      F1 is widely used in NLP benchmarks, anomaly detection, and information retrieval. It's a fine summary number but it loses information that precision-recall curves preserve.
    pitfalls:
      - "F1 weighs precision and recall equally. If you actually care more about one than the other, use the more general F-beta score (beta > 1 weighs recall higher, beta < 1 weighs precision)."
  - question: "What is a ROC curve, and what does its AUC mean?"
    shortAnswer: "The ROC curve plots true-positive rate against false-positive rate as you sweep the threshold. AUC (area under the curve) summarizes overall ranking quality — between 0.5 (random) and 1.0 (perfect). Higher = better."
    detailed: |
      True positive rate (= recall) is on the y-axis; false positive rate (FP / actual negatives) is on the x-axis. As you lower the threshold, you move up and right. A perfect classifier hugs the top-left corner; a random classifier traces the diagonal.

      AUC interpretation: 'if you randomly pick one positive and one negative sample, what's the probability the model ranks the positive higher?' AUC of 0.8 means 80% of such pairs are ranked correctly. AUC is threshold-independent — it measures the model's overall ranking quality, not its performance at any specific threshold.
    pitfalls:
      - "For highly imbalanced datasets, ROC-AUC can look great while the model is practically useless. Use Precision-Recall AUC (PR-AUC) for imbalanced problems."
  - question: "What's the difference between ROC-AUC and PR-AUC?"
    shortAnswer: "ROC-AUC plots TPR vs FPR — both normalized. It's relatively stable across class imbalance. PR-AUC plots Precision vs Recall — Precision depends on class balance, so PR-AUC is more revealing for imbalanced problems."
    detailed: |
      On a 99/1 imbalanced dataset, even a poor model gets a respectable ROC-AUC because FPR has a giant denominator (lots of negatives) that washes out errors. PR-AUC, by contrast, exposes the model's failures sharply — when the positive class is rare, precision is dominated by false positives even if FPR looks fine.

      Rule of thumb: balanced data → ROC-AUC is fine. Imbalanced data (rarer than 10% positive) → use PR-AUC and report it alongside.
    pitfalls:
      - "Don't compare ROC-AUC scores across datasets with different class balances — they aren't on the same scale."
  - question: "What is a confusion matrix?"
    shortAnswer: "A 2×2 table for binary classifiers showing the four possible outcomes: true positives, false positives, true negatives, false negatives. Every other metric is a one-or-two-number summary of this table."
    detailed: |
      ```
                          Actual Positive      Actual Negative
      Predicted Positive  True Positive (TP)   False Positive (FP)
      Predicted Negative  False Negative (FN)  True Negative (TN)
      ```

      Once you have these four numbers, every metric is just a ratio:
      - Accuracy = (TP + TN) / total
      - Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
      - Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
      - F1 = 2 · P · R / (P + R)

      The confusion matrix is the ground truth — always look at it before relying on any summary metric. It often reveals failure modes that summary numbers hide.
    pitfalls:
      - "For multi-class problems, the confusion matrix is N×N — it shows not just whether you got it wrong but *what* you got it wrong as. Very informative for debugging."

<TldrCallout>
  *Accuracy* is the fraction of correct predictions — useful only when classes are balanced.
  *Precision* and *recall* are a trade-off: precision is the quality of positive predictions, recall
  is the coverage of real positives. *F1* averages them. *ROC-AUC* and *PR-AUC* summarize
  performance across all thresholds. A *confusion matrix* shows the raw numbers behind every other
  metric. Picking the right one matters more than picking the model that scores highest on the wrong
  one.
</TldrCallout>

## Why this lesson matters

Picking the wrong metric is how teams ship bad models that look good on
paper. The most common version: a team reports 99% accuracy on a
fraud-detection model, deploys it, and discovers it catches almost no
fraud — because it was a 99/1 imbalanced dataset and the model learned the
shortcut "always say not-fraud."

We're going to build the vocabulary to never make that mistake.

## The confusion matrix is the source of truth

For any binary classifier, every prediction falls into one of four buckets:

```
                       Actual: Positive       Actual: Negative
Predicted: Positive    True Positive (TP)     False Positive (FP)
Predicted: Negative    False Negative (FN)    True Negative (TN)
```

A{" "}

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Confusion Matrix"
  definition="A 2×2 table (for binary classification) showing the count of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives. The raw foundation under every binary-classification metric. For multi-class problems, it generalizes to N×N."
>
  confusion matrix
</GlossaryTerm>
records the counts in each bucket. Every summary metric you'll read about is built from those four
numbers.

## Accuracy, and why it lies

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Accuracy"
  definition="The fraction of predictions that are correct. (TP + TN) / total. Reliable only when classes are roughly balanced and false positives and false negatives are equally bad."
>
  Accuracy
</GlossaryTerm>
is the fraction of correct predictions: `(TP + TN) / total`. It's intuitive and the most-cited
metric in beginner ML — and it's the most-misleading metric in real ML.

The reason: when the classes are imbalanced, you can score high accuracy
by ignoring the rare class entirely.

<WorkedExample title="Accuracy lies on an imbalanced fraud dataset">

A bank has 100,000 transactions; 1,000 of them are fraud (1% fraud rate). We
build a 'classifier' that always predicts not-fraud.

Confusion matrix:

```
                         Actual: Fraud (1,000)  Actual: Not-fraud (99,000)
Predicted: Fraud         0                       0
Predicted: Not-fraud     1,000                   99,000
```

Accuracy = (0 + 99,000) / 100,000 = **99%**.

Sounds great. The classifier is catching exactly zero fraud and is
completely useless.

The fix: use a metric that pays attention to the positive class. Recall on
fraud here is 0/1000 = 0%. That's the honest summary.

</WorkedExample>

## Precision and recall: a trade-off

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Precision"
  definition="Of the samples the model predicted positive, the fraction that are actually positive. TP / (TP + FP). High precision means 'when the model says yes, it's usually right'. Matters most when false positives are expensive."
>
  Precision
</GlossaryTerm>
asks "when the model says positive, how often is it right?":

```
Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
```

<GlossaryTerm
  term="Recall"
  definition="Of all actual positive samples, the fraction the model correctly identified. TP / (TP + FN). High recall means 'the model catches most of the real positives'. Matters most when false negatives are expensive."
>
  Recall
</GlossaryTerm>
asks "of all the real positives, how many did the model catch?":

```
Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
```

They trade off because most classifiers internally output a probability,
and the yes/no decision comes from a _threshold_. Lower the threshold and
you flag more samples — recall goes up (you catch more real positives) but
precision goes down (you also flag more not-really-positives). Raise the
threshold and the opposite happens.

The right balance depends on what each kind of error costs:

- **Cancer screening:** false negatives are catastrophic (miss a real case).
  Favor recall — accept more false alarms to ensure nothing slips through.
- **Spam filtering:** false positives are annoying (legitimate email
  blocked). Favor precision — better to let some spam through than block a
  job offer.
- **Fraud detection:** depends on policy. If a positive freezes the
  account, false positives are very costly (favor precision). If a positive
  just triggers a "verify this transaction?" prompt, favor recall.

## F1 — one number when you must have one

<GlossaryTerm
  term="F1 Score"
  definition="The harmonic mean of precision and recall: 2·P·R / (P+R). A single number summary that punishes models with very unbalanced precision and recall. Used heavily in NLP and information retrieval."
>
  F1
</GlossaryTerm>
is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:

```
F1 = 2 · P · R / (P + R)
```

The harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller of P and R, so F1 only
rewards models that are good at both. It's a useful single-number summary
— but you should always also look at P and R separately to know _how_ the
model is balanced.

## ROC and AUC — performance across all thresholds

A{" "}

<GlossaryTerm
  term="ROC Curve"
  definition="Receiver Operating Characteristic. Plots true-positive rate (recall) on the y-axis against false-positive rate on the x-axis as the decision threshold varies. A perfect classifier hugs the top-left corner; a random one traces the diagonal."
>
  ROC curve
</GlossaryTerm>
sweeps the decision threshold from 0 to 1, plotting true-positive rate (recall) against
false-positive rate at each point. A perfect classifier reaches the top-left corner (100% TPR with
0% FPR); a random classifier traces the diagonal.

<GlossaryTerm
  term="AUC"
  definition="Area Under the (ROC) Curve. A single number between 0.5 (random) and 1.0 (perfect) summarizing classifier quality across all thresholds. Interpretation: the probability that a randomly chosen positive sample is ranked higher than a randomly chosen negative."
>
  AUC
</GlossaryTerm>
is the area under the ROC curve. 0.5 is random, 1.0 is perfect. Common loose target: 0.8+ is
"decent", 0.9+ is "good".

AUC has a clean probabilistic interpretation: if you pick a random
positive sample and a random negative sample, AUC is the probability that
the model ranks the positive one higher.

## PR-AUC vs ROC-AUC for imbalanced data

ROC-AUC has a subtle bias: on heavily imbalanced datasets, the FPR
denominator (number of actual negatives) is huge, which makes the FPR
small even when the model produces many false positives. The result is a
deceptively high ROC-AUC for a practically useless model.

For imbalanced problems (rarer than ~10% positive), use the
**precision-recall curve** and its area, **PR-AUC**, instead. PR-AUC
exposes the model's failures more honestly when positives are rare.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Imagine grading a job-application filter. Accuracy says "99% of the resumes you correctly
  handled." Precision says "of the resumes you said 'great candidate', how many actually were?"
  Recall says "of the great candidates in the pile, how many did you flag?" The right summary
  depends on whether you're afraid of bothering interviewers with bad candidates (precision) or
  letting great candidates slip away (recall).
</AnalogyCallout>

## Where to look first when evaluating a model

A practical order of operations:

1. **Always look at the confusion matrix first.** The summary metrics make
   sense only once you've seen the raw counts.
2. **Check the class balance.** If imbalanced, ignore accuracy. Use
   PR-AUC, plus precision and recall at your chosen threshold.
3. **Decide what your error costs look like** (false positives vs. false
   negatives). Pick the metric — or threshold — that reflects that
   asymmetry.
4. **Don't celebrate yet.** Check failure modes. Misclassified examples
   often reveal feature bugs, label noise, or distribution gaps that no
   summary metric exposes.
