importantQuestions:
  - question: "Why does weight initialisation matter so much?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Initial weights determine the variance of activations and gradients at every layer. Bad init → activations blow up or vanish through depth, gradients follow, training fails before it starts.
    detailed: |
      For a layer z = Wx with W initialised with variance σ_W², input variance σ_x², the output variance is roughly n · σ_W² · σ_x² where n is the input dimension. If σ_W² is too large, activations grow with depth; too small, they shrink. Through L layers, multiplicative effects compound dramatically.

      **Xavier / Glorot init** (Glorot & Bengio, 2010): Var(W) = 2 / (n_in + n_out). Keeps activations roughly constant through the network for sigmoid / tanh activations.

      **He / Kaiming init** (He et al., 2015): Var(W) = 2 / n_in. Tuned for ReLU, which kills half the units on average. Without this correction, ReLU networks underflow within ~10 layers.

      Default in PyTorch: `nn.Linear` uses Kaiming uniform by default. `nn.Conv2d` same. If you write a custom layer, initialise explicitly.
    pitfalls:
      - "For SwiGLU and other gated activations, init is a research topic. Most modern LLMs use a custom init derived empirically."
  - question: "What does Batch Normalization actually do?"
    shortAnswer: |
      For each feature (channel), normalise to zero mean and unit variance using batch statistics, then apply a learned affine transform. Stabilises training, allows higher learning rates, mild regularising effect.
    detailed: |
      For activation z ∈ ℝ^(B × C × ...):

      1. Compute mean μ and variance σ² across batch dim for each channel.
      2. Normalise: ẑ = (z − μ) / √(σ² + ε).
      3. Affine: y = γẑ + β, where γ and β are learnable per-channel parameters.

      At training, batch stats are used. At inference, running averages of training stats (maintained by moving average during training) are used. The train/eval distinction matters — forgetting model.eval() at inference uses the current minibatch stats and breaks predictions.

      Effects:
      - Stabilises forward pass (constant variance per layer).
      - Allows higher learning rates.
      - Slight regularising effect (batch dependency adds noise).
      - Breaks if batch size is very small (1–4) — batch stats too noisy.

      Introduced by Ioffe & Szegedy (2015); the explanation in the paper ("internal covariate shift") is now considered partial — the actual benefit is more about loss landscape smoothing.
    pitfalls:
      - "BatchNorm with batch size 1 is broken — variance is undefined. Use LayerNorm or GroupNorm for small batches."
  - question: "When should you use LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm?"
    shortAnswer: |
      LayerNorm normalises across feature dimensions per example, independent of batch. Used in transformers, RNNs, anywhere batch size varies or sequence dimension dominates. Default for any non-CNN architecture.
    detailed: |
      LayerNorm (Ba, Kiros, Hinton, 2016) normalises each example individually:

      For activation z ∈ ℝ^(B × D), compute mean and variance over the D dim, per example.

      Advantages over BatchNorm:
      - No batch dependency. Works with batch size 1.
      - Works for variable sequence lengths (transformers, RNNs).
      - Same behaviour train and eval (no running averages).

      In 2026: BatchNorm dominates CNNs (vision); LayerNorm dominates transformers (NLP, multimodal). Some recent vision models (ConvNeXt) use LayerNorm even in convolutional contexts.

      Variants: **RMSNorm** (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) drops the mean subtraction and bias, keeping only the rescaling — cheaper and comparable in quality, now used in Llama, Mistral, and other modern LLMs.
    pitfalls:
      - "GroupNorm sits between LayerNorm and BatchNorm: normalise within groups of channels, per example. Useful when channel structure matters and batch is small."
  - question: "How does dropout work, and is it still relevant?"
    shortAnswer: |
      During training, randomly set each activation to zero with probability p (typically 0.1–0.5). Scales remaining activations by 1/(1−p) to maintain expected value. At inference, no dropout — use full network.
    detailed: |
      Srivastava et al. (2014). The intuition: prevent neurons from co-adapting (relying on specific other neurons being present). The math: trains an ensemble of exponentially many sub-networks with shared weights; inference approximates the ensemble average.

      In 2026:
      - Still standard in vision CNNs (dropout=0.1–0.2 in fully connected heads).
      - Still standard in transformers (attention dropout, FFN dropout, residual dropout).
      - Rarely the dominant regulariser. Data augmentation and weight decay matter more.

      Variants: **DropPath** (stochastic depth) drops entire residual blocks instead of individual activations — used in modern vision transformers. **AttentionDropout** drops elements of the attention weight matrix.

      Strong opinion: don't tune dropout heavily. Set it to 0.1 and move on. The gains from careful dropout tuning are smaller than from architecture or data choices.
    pitfalls:
      - "Inverted dropout (scaling at training time) is standard now; original dropout scaled at inference. Both equivalent; modern frameworks use inverted."
  - question: "What's weight decay and how does it relate to L2 regularisation?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Weight decay penalises ‖W‖² in the loss, equivalent to L2 regularisation. Standard for neural networks; default coefficient 1e-4 to 1e-2. AdamW separates weight decay from the optimiser step.
    detailed: |
      Plain SGD with L2: L_total = L_data + λ‖W‖². Gradient: ∇L_data + 2λW. Update: W ← W − η∇L_data − 2ηλW. The 2ηλW term "decays" the weights toward zero each step.

      Adam with L2: the decay term gets normalised by the gradient magnitude estimate (RMS), which makes the effective regularisation hyperparameter-dependent. AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) decouples weight decay from the gradient normalisation:

      W ← W − η · m̂/(√v̂ + ε) − η · λ · W

      AdamW is now the default optimiser for transformers; the decoupling matters more than the math suggests.

      Typical λ for transformers: 0.01–0.1. For CNNs: 1e-4. Smaller models tolerate less regularisation; large models need more.
    pitfalls:
      - "Don't apply weight decay to biases or normalisation parameters. PyTorch's AdamW lets you exclude them via parameter groups."
  - question: "Why does training a deep network suddenly work in the 2010s?"
    shortAnswer: |
      The combination: ReLU + Xavier/He init + (eventually) BatchNorm + residual connections + dropout + Adam. No single piece was sufficient; together they removed every obstacle to training depth.
    detailed: |
      Pre-2010 obstacles:
      - Vanishing gradients (sigmoid + bad init) — ReLU + He init.
      - Activation drift across layers — BatchNorm / LayerNorm.
      - Overfit on small data — dropout, weight decay, augmentation.
      - Slow / unstable convergence — Adam.
      - Depth itself blowing up — residual connections (Module 5).

      Each was solved separately, and the assembled stack lets you train networks with hundreds (or thousands) of layers reliably. None of the individual ideas is conceptually deep; the engineering together is what changed.

      For a modern transformer training script: He init for linear/conv weights, LayerNorm or RMSNorm, AdamW with weight decay 0.01, no dropout (modern LLMs often disable it), residual connections everywhere. The recipe has converged.
    pitfalls:
      - "Modern LLMs sometimes turn off dropout entirely. Sufficient data and scale handle the regularisation."

## Initialisation: don't start with N(0, 1)

A deep network's forward pass is a chain of matrix multiplies and non-linearities. The variance of activations and gradients depends on the initialisation; bad init makes them explode or vanish through depth.

**Xavier / Glorot init** (2010) for sigmoid / tanh:

Var(W_ij) = 2 / (n_in + n_out)

**He / Kaiming init** (2015) for ReLU:

Var(W_ij) = 2 / n_in

The factor of 2 in He init compensates for ReLU killing half the activations on average. Without it, even a 10-layer ReLU network has activations collapsing toward zero by the output.

In 2026 PyTorch: `nn.Linear` and `nn.Conv2d` use He / Kaiming uniform by default. You don't usually call init explicitly — it happens at construction. For custom layers, use `torch.nn.init.kaiming_normal_` or similar.

## Normalisation: keep activations sane through training

Even with good init, training can drive activation distributions away from the init regime as weights change. **Batch Normalization** (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015) fixes this by re-normalising activations every layer.

For activation z (shape: batch × features):

1. Compute mean μ and variance σ² over batch dimension, per feature.
2. Normalise: ẑ = (z − μ) / √(σ² + ε).
3. Affine: y = γẑ + β, where γ and β are learnable per-feature.

Benefits:

- Stabilises forward pass (constant variance per layer).
- Permits 10–100× higher learning rates.
- Mild regularising effect (batch dependency adds noise).
- Empirically: makes deep networks trainable that weren't before.

Limitations:

- Breaks at batch size 1–4 (statistics are unreliable).
- Sequence-length-dependent (which transformers hate).
- Train/eval discrepancy: training uses batch stats; eval uses running averages. Forgetting `model.eval()` ruins predictions.

## LayerNorm and RMSNorm: what transformers actually use

**LayerNorm** (Ba, Kiros, Hinton, 2016) normalises across features per example instead of across batch:

For z (shape: batch × ... × features), compute mean and variance over the last dim, per example.

Advantages: no batch dependency, identical train/eval behaviour, works for variable sequence lengths. Standard in transformers, RNNs, everything non-CNN.

**RMSNorm** (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) is a cheaper variant — drops the mean subtraction and bias, just rescales by RMS:

ẑ = z / √(mean(z²) + ε), y = γ · ẑ

Used in Llama, Mistral, and most modern open LLMs. Roughly half the compute of LayerNorm, comparable quality at scale.

| Architecture                                         | Normalisation          |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| CNNs (vision)                                        | BatchNorm              |
| Classical transformers (BERT, GPT-2/3)               | LayerNorm              |
| Modern LLMs (Llama, Mistral, PaLM)                   | RMSNorm                |
| Small batch / RNN                                    | LayerNorm or GroupNorm |
| Variable batch (object detection at multiple scales) | GroupNorm              |

## Dropout and weight decay: the regularisers

**Dropout** (Srivastava et al., 2014): during training, randomly zero out each activation with probability p, scale survivors by 1/(1−p). At inference, no dropout. The intuition: prevents neurons from co-adapting; trains an implicit ensemble of sub-networks.

Typical p:

- Vision CNN FC head: 0.5 (heavy)
- Transformer attention / FFN: 0.1
- Modern LLM training: often 0 (data scale handles regularisation)

**Weight decay** (L2 regularisation): penalise ‖W‖² in the loss. Standard λ:

- Vision CNN: 1e-4
- Transformer: 0.01–0.1
- LLM (AdamW): 0.01–0.1

**AdamW** (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) decouples weight decay from Adam's gradient normalisation — the correct way to combine Adam with regularisation. The default optimiser for transformers in 2026.

**Early stopping**: stop training when validation loss stops improving. Free regulariser; always worth doing.

These four — dropout, weight decay, early stopping, data augmentation — are the regularisation toolkit. Combine them; tune the strongest one for your problem.

## The standard recipe

For a from-scratch deep network in 2026:

| Choice         | Default                                                                                    |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Init           | He / Kaiming (default in PyTorch nn.Linear, nn.Conv2d)                                     |
| Normalisation  | BatchNorm for CNN; LayerNorm or RMSNorm for transformer                                    |
| Regularisation | Weight decay 0.01 (AdamW), light dropout (0.1 in transformer attention/FFN, 0.5 in CNN FC) |
| Optimizer      | AdamW (A4.5)                                                                               |
| Scheduler      | Cosine or linear with warmup (A4.7)                                                        |

This is the converged starting point. Deviations are research questions, not defaults.

## When to deviate

- **Very deep networks** (50+ layers): use residual connections (Module 5) plus everything above.
- **Very large batches** (>1024): consider Layer-wise Adaptive Rate Scaling (LARS, LAMB).
- **Very small batches** (1–4): switch BatchNorm to GroupNorm or LayerNorm.
- **Reinforcement learning** (non-stationary data): often disable BatchNorm; use LayerNorm.
- **Modern LLM training**: often disable dropout entirely; scale handles it.

## Closing notes

These three families of tricks — init, normalisation, regularisation — are the unsexy infrastructure that makes deep learning work. The headlines go to architectures (transformers, residual connections); the trenches are these.

For interview-style understanding: be able to derive why He init has the factor of 2, explain BatchNorm's train/eval distinction, and articulate why LayerNorm beats BatchNorm in transformers. These are the questions that separate "knows the recipe" from "knows what each ingredient does."
