importantQuestions:
  - question: "What is a residual connection, in one equation?"
    shortAnswer: |
      y = x + f(x), where f is a learnable block (e.g., a few conv layers + activation). The block learns the residual (delta) over the input, not the full transformation.
    detailed: |
      He et al. (2015) introduced residual blocks in ResNet, winning ImageNet that year. The block:

      y = x + f(x)

      where x is the input, f is a small block (typically 2-3 layers with a final ReLU). The key change vs. plain stacking: f only has to learn the delta from x to y, not y itself.

      Why this matters:
      - **Gradient flow.** ∂y/∂x = 1 + ∂f/∂x. The "1" provides a direct gradient path that doesn't compound through the block. Vanishing gradients across many blocks largely disappear.
      - **Identity initialisation.** If f initialises to zero, y = x — the block is harmless at start. Easier optimisation.
      - **Depth becomes free.** ResNet trained 150-layer networks successfully when 20-layer plain networks couldn't. Modern transformers use 50–100+ blocks.

      Every modern architecture — ResNet, Transformer, U-Net, ViT, ConvNeXt — uses residual connections in some form. Without them, depth doesn't pay.
    pitfalls:
      - "If f's output shape differs from x's, you need a 1×1 projection on the skip path. ResNet handles this transparently."
  - question: "Why doesn't plain deep stacking work?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Past ~20 layers, vanilla networks (pre-ResNet) suffered from vanishing gradients, harder optimisation, and an empirical degradation where deeper networks performed worse on training set, not better. Skip connections solved all three.
    detailed: |
      Three problems past depth ~20:

      1. **Vanishing gradients.** Each layer's Jacobian is multiplied through; for ReLU + good init, the product still shrinks. Gradients reaching early layers are tiny.

      2. **Optimisation difficulty.** Even with valid gradients, the loss landscape gets harder. Plain deep networks routinely failed to converge.

      3. **Degradation.** He et al. observed: 56-layer plain CNN had HIGHER training error than 20-layer plain CNN. This wasn't overfitting (training error worse, not test) — deeper plain networks were *worse-optimisable*, not just less generalisable.

      Residual connections fixed all three: gradient has a direct path back, identity init makes the block harmless to start, and the optimization landscape becomes smoother.

      In 2026, no production architecture goes deep without skip connections. The 20-layer plain network is a curiosity.
    pitfalls:
      - "Residual connections don't directly fix overfitting. They make depth trainable; depth still needs regularisation and data."
  - question: "How do residual connections work in transformers?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Each sub-layer (attention or feedforward) is wrapped with x + sublayer(x), plus normalisation. Pre-norm vs. post-norm differs in where the LayerNorm sits.
    detailed: |
      **Original transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) — post-norm:**

      ```
      y = LayerNorm(x + Attention(x))
      z = LayerNorm(y + FFN(y))
      ```

      The norm is applied *after* the residual sum.

      **Modern LLMs — pre-norm:**

      ```
      y = x + Attention(LayerNorm(x))
      z = y + FFN(LayerNorm(y))
      ```

      The norm is applied *before* the sub-layer; the skip path bypasses norm entirely.

      Why the switch? Post-norm transformers needed careful learning rate warmup to train stably at depth. Pre-norm transformers train more stably with simpler schedules. Almost all modern LLMs (GPT-2 onwards, Llama, Mistral, T5) use pre-norm. The original post-norm survives mostly in BERT-style models.

      Pre-norm also tends to produce smaller gradients in the residual path — useful for very deep networks but can lead to under-trained sub-layers if not balanced.
    pitfalls:
      - "There's an ongoing debate about which is genuinely better. Pre-norm is the practical default; specific tasks may benefit from post-norm with careful tuning."
  - question: "What's a U-Net, and how does it use skip connections?"
    shortAnswer: |
      An encoder-decoder that downsamples then upsamples, with skip connections from each encoder level to the corresponding decoder level. Used in image segmentation (medical, satellite), and as the backbone of diffusion models.
    detailed: |
      U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) was designed for medical image segmentation. Structure:

      1. **Encoder**: standard CNN, downsampling spatial resolution while increasing channels.
      2. **Decoder**: upsampling (transposed conv or interpolation) back to input resolution.
      3. **Skip connections**: at each spatial scale, the encoder activation is concatenated (not added) with the decoder activation.

      Why skips matter here: the encoder loses spatial precision through downsampling. The decoder needs that fine detail to produce precise segmentation masks. The skips reintroduce high-resolution features at each upsampling stage.

      In 2026 U-Net is the standard backbone for:
      - Medical image segmentation (still).
      - Semantic / instance segmentation in general (with modifications).
      - **Diffusion models** (Stable Diffusion, Imagen, DALL-E, almost all image generators). The U-Net's symmetry matches the denoising process. ViT-based diffusion (DiT) is gaining ground but U-Net dominates production.

      A different skip-connection pattern (concatenation, not addition) than ResNet but the same principle: don't lose information unnecessarily through depth.
    pitfalls:
      - "U-Net skips concatenate (cat); ResNet skips add. Different shapes downstream."
  - question: "Does this affect transformer training in practice?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Yes — pre-norm vs. post-norm changes whether you need long warmup, and which initialisation works. Practical setups are converged: pre-norm + RMSNorm + AdamW + cosine schedule with short warmup.
    detailed: |
      A modern transformer training recipe in 2026 includes:

      - **Pre-norm** residual structure.
      - **RMSNorm** or LayerNorm.
      - **Residual scaling** at deep models — sometimes the residual contribution is scaled by 1/√L where L is depth, to keep activation magnitudes stable. Common in GPT-3 and beyond.
      - **Init**: small init in deep models (1/√(layer_depth) scaling) to avoid initial blowup.

      Without these, training 100+ layer transformers becomes flaky. With them, it's routine — and the same training script scales from 100M to 70B parameters with minor adjustments.

      The earliest deep-transformer papers (GPT-2, GPT-3) had to figure out these tricks empirically. By 2026 the recipe is open-sourced (look at Llama, Mistral training configs) and reusable.
    pitfalls:
      - "Don't over-scale residuals. If you scale too aggressively, the network becomes 'effectively' shallower and underuses its parameter count."
  - question: "Is ResNet still relevant in 2026?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Yes — ResNet-50 and ResNet-101 are still the canonical 'baseline strong vision backbone' for many production tasks. ConvNeXt and ViT outperform them at scale, but ResNet remains a healthy default for small-data and mid-data regimes.
    detailed: |
      Reasons ResNet endures:

      - **Well-trained pretrained checkpoints** are everywhere — every major framework ships ResNet-50 ImageNet weights.
      - **Strong baselines.** New architectures get compared to "ResNet-50 fine-tuned for the same number of epochs."
      - **Production maturity.** Many vision systems shipped with ResNet, and the maintenance / debugging knowledge is broad.
      - **Computational efficiency.** ResNet-50 runs at 100+ FPS on consumer GPUs at 224×224; reasonable for many production constraints.

      What's faded:
      - **From-scratch CNN architecture research.** Most new architectures (ConvNeXt, Swin, etc.) are improvements over ResNet, not replacements.
      - **Large-scale image classification** — ViT-style models dominate.

      In 2026, a reasonable vision baseline is: pretrained ResNet-50 + fine-tuned head. Beats most from-scratch approaches on most realistic data.
    pitfalls:
      - "Pretrained ResNets from different sources differ subtly. Document which weights you're starting from."

<TldrCallout>
  Residual connections — y = x + f(x) — are the single architectural change that made very deep
  networks (100+ layers) trainable. They provide a direct gradient path through depth, make the
  optimisation landscape smoother, and allow safe identity initialisation. Every modern architecture
  (ResNet, ViT, transformer-LLM, U-Net diffusion) uses them.
</TldrCallout>

## The pre-residual depth wall

Before 2015, deep CNNs ran into a depth wall around layer 20-30. Vanishing gradients, harder optimisation, and an empirically observed "degradation" — deeper plain networks had _worse training error_, not just worse generalisation.

ResNet (He et al., 2015) won ImageNet with a 152-layer network by introducing one change: skip connections.

The trick is one equation:

y = x + f(x)

Each block adds its input to its output. The block learns the _residual_ (delta) over the input, not the full transformation.

## Why this works

Three reasons, all important:

**1. Gradient flow.** ∂y/∂x = 1 + ∂f/∂x. The constant "1" is a direct gradient path that doesn't compound through f. Through L blocks, the gradient that reaches the input has an unobstructed component, not just the product of L Jacobians.

**2. Identity initialisation.** Initialise f's parameters near zero. Then y ≈ x at start — the block doesn't break anything. Training learns small deltas on top of identity; much easier to optimise than learning the full transformation from scratch.

**3. Loss landscape.** Empirically (Li et al., 2018), residual networks have visibly smoother loss landscapes than plain networks. Optimisation is easier in addition to gradient flow.

All three together — gradient path, init, landscape — turn depth from a liability into a free lunch. Modern transformers stack 70+ residual blocks routinely.

<AnalogyCallout>
  Think of a delivery courier. Plain network: rebuild the entire address from scratch at each stop.
  Residual network: keep the existing address and add a small correction at each stop. The latter is
  faster, more reliable, and tolerates many more stops without confusion.
</AnalogyCallout>

## Variants: how the skip is wired

**ResNet basic block (CNN):**

```
x → Conv → BN → ReLU → Conv → BN → + → ReLU → y
                                     ↑
                                     x
```

The skip path bypasses the inner conv layers; the addition happens before the final ReLU.

**Transformer pre-norm:**

```
x → LN → Attention → + → ...
                     ↑
                     x
```

Norm before the sub-layer; skip is unnormalised.

**Transformer post-norm (original):**

```
x → Attention → + → LN → ...
                ↑
                x
```

Norm after the addition; the skip goes through norm.

**U-Net concatenation skip:**

```
encoder activation at scale s → cat → decoder activation at scale s
```

Concatenation along channel dim, not addition. Used in segmentation and diffusion U-Nets.

## Pre-norm vs. post-norm in transformers

The original "Attention is All You Need" (2017) used post-norm. It worked but required careful warmup and was finicky to scale beyond ~12 layers.

Modern LLMs (GPT-2 onwards, Llama, Mistral, T5) use pre-norm. Trains more stably at depth; simpler schedule.

| Pre-norm                               | Post-norm                          |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Standard in modern LLMs                | Standard in BERT-era models        |
| Stable at depth (50+)                  | Needs warmup; flaky at large depth |
| Slightly worse final quality (debated) | Slightly better in some benchmarks |
| Easier to train                        | Harder to train                    |

In 2026: pre-norm is the production default. Use post-norm only if you have a specific reason and the engineering budget for careful tuning.

## Residual scaling at extreme depth

For very deep transformers (50+ layers), even residual connections can let activations grow with depth. Modern practice scales the residual contribution:

y = x + α · sublayer(x), with α = 1/√L or similar.

Used in GPT-3, scaling laws papers, modern LLMs. Keeps activation magnitudes constant at init across arbitrary depth, allowing 100+ layer transformers without retuning.

## What residual connections are not

- **Not regularisation.** They change what the network computes, not just how it trains.
- **Not memory.** ResNet-50 takes more memory than VGG-16 because of the activation skip paths to be retained for backward.
- **Not a substitute for normalization.** BatchNorm / LayerNorm + residuals together made depth tractable. Either alone is weaker.

## Skip patterns across architectures

| Architecture       | Skip type     | Connection                                           |
| ------------------ | ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| ResNet             | Additive      | Skip every 2-3 conv layers                           |
| DenseNet           | Concatenative | All previous layers feed into each layer (expensive) |
| U-Net              | Concatenative | Symmetric encoder ↔ decoder at each scale            |
| Transformer (any)  | Additive      | Around each attention and FFN sub-layer              |
| Vision Transformer | Additive      | Same as transformer                                  |
| Diffusion U-Net    | Both          | U-Net concat skips + ResBlock additive skips inside  |

The common idea: don't lose information through depth. Different domains use different specific patterns but the principle is unchanged since 2015.

## The depth revolution in one paragraph

Before 2015: depth was hard because gradients vanished and optimisation was fragile. ResNet's residual connections gave gradients a direct path and made each block harmless at initialisation. Every architecture since — ConvNeXt, Vision Transformer, U-Net, GPT, BERT, Llama — uses some form of skip connection. The "deep" in deep learning is conditional on this single architectural primitive working.
