importantQuestions:
  - question: "What does a convolution layer actually do?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Slides a learnable filter (kernel) across the spatial dimensions of the input, computing a weighted sum at each position. Output channels are produced from different filters; spatial structure is preserved.
    detailed: |
      For input X ∈ ℝ^(C_in × H × W) and a kernel K ∈ ℝ^(C_out × C_in × k × k):

      Y[c_out, h, w] = Σ_{c_in, i, j} K[c_out, c_in, i, j] · X[c_in, h + i, w + j] + b[c_out]

      That's a dot product between the kernel and a k × k patch of the input, summed over input channels. Slide the kernel by stride s across the entire input.

      Output spatial shape: ⌊(H + 2p − k) / s⌋ + 1, same formula for width.

      Why convolutions instead of fully connected layers for images:

      1. **Translation equivariance** — moving the input shifts the output the same way. Useful because cats look like cats no matter where they appear in the image.
      2. **Parameter efficiency** — the same kernel applies everywhere. A 3×3 conv on 64 → 64 channels has 64 · 64 · 9 = 36,864 weights. An FC layer between the same activations on a 224 × 224 image would have ~3.2 billion weights.
      3. **Inductive bias** — local connectivity matches the structure of natural images, where nearby pixels are correlated.

      LeCun's 1989 CNN (handwritten digit recognition) introduced these ideas; AlexNet (2012) scaled them up; ResNet (2015) made them deep. The fundamentals haven't changed.
    pitfalls:
      - "Strictly: convolution in deep learning is cross-correlation (no kernel flipping). The math is the same in the symmetric case but matters in some derivations."
  - question: "What's the difference between convolution, stride, padding, and pooling?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Convolution: the weighted-sum operation. Stride: how far the kernel moves per step (larger = smaller output). Padding: pixels added around the input (preserves spatial size). Pooling: non-learned downsampling (max or average over patches).
    detailed: |
      **Stride.** Stride 1: kernel moves one pixel at a time, output ≈ input size. Stride 2: output is half size. Stride > 1 is the modern way to downsample (replaces pooling in many architectures).

      **Padding.** Add p pixels of zeros around input. Padding 'same' keeps spatial dim constant (for k=3 → p=1, k=5 → p=2). Padding 'valid' means no padding, output shrinks by k−1 per side.

      **Pooling.** Max pool (typically 2×2 stride 2): take the max of each 2×2 patch. Average pool: take the mean. Downsamples by 2× spatially, no learnable parameters.

      Modern architectures usually replace max pooling with strided convolutions (learnable downsampling) and use global average pooling at the end (replaces flatten + FC).
    pitfalls:
      - "Dilation (atrous convolutions) inflate the receptive field without more parameters. Used in segmentation (DeepLab) and sometimes detection."
  - question: "How does a CNN see the whole image despite using small (3×3) kernels?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Stack many conv layers. Each layer's output 'sees' a 3×3 patch of the previous layer, which itself sees a 5×5 patch of the input. After L layers (with downsampling), the receptive field grows to cover most of the image.
    detailed: |
      Receptive field after one 3×3 conv: 3×3. After two: 5×5. After L convs with stride 1: (2L+1) × (2L+1). With downsampling (stride 2 every few layers), the receptive field grows much faster.

      A typical ResNet-50 has a final receptive field of ~200 pixels — covering most of a 224×224 input. The last layers reason about the whole image; the first layers reason about edges and textures.

      This hierarchical view is why CNNs work: early layers extract local features (edges, colours); middle layers compose them (shapes, textures); late layers reason semantically (object parts, then objects). The hierarchy is implicit in the architecture, not learned from explicit supervision.

      Vision Transformers replace this with global self-attention from layer 1, but pay a quadratic cost in image patches.
    pitfalls:
      - "Receptive field is theoretical; the *effective* receptive field is usually smaller because some paths through the network contribute more than others (Luo et al., 2016)."
  - question: "When should you use ResNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, or ViT?"
    shortAnswer: |
      ResNet for legacy compat and strong baselines. EfficientNet for mobile / compute-constrained. ConvNeXt for modern CNN performance. ViT for large data + compute. In 2026 most production starts with pretrained foundation backbones (CLIP, DINOv2) regardless of architecture.
    detailed: |
      **ResNet** (He et al., 2015). Residual connections, BatchNorm, stride-2 downsampling. Trained well; understood deeply. Standard 'reasonable strong baseline.' Used everywhere as feature extractors.

      **EfficientNet** (Tan & Le, 2019). Compound scaling of depth/width/resolution. Better accuracy per FLOP than ResNet. Used in mobile + edge deployment.

      **ConvNeXt** (Liu et al., 2022). 'Modernised' CNN — adopts ideas from transformers (LayerNorm, GELU, large kernels). Matches ViT performance while keeping CNN inductive bias. Strong on medium data.

      **Vision Transformer (ViT)** (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020). Splits image into patches, treats as a sequence, applies a transformer. Beats CNNs at large scale; underperforms on small data without heavy augmentation.

      In 2026 production: most projects start from a pretrained backbone — CLIP for image-text, DINOv2 for self-supervised features, ImageNet-pretrained ConvNeXt or EfficientNet for direct fine-tuning. From-scratch training on small data is rare and rarely competitive.
    pitfalls:
      - "ViT needs ~100M+ images to outperform ResNet. On ImageNet alone, modern CNNs (ConvNeXt) match or beat ViT."
  - question: "What's the standard CNN training recipe in 2026?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Pretrained backbone → replace head → fine-tune. Augmentation: random crop + flip + colour jitter + Mixup + CutMix + RandAugment. Optimizer: AdamW (or SGD+momentum for some recipes). Schedule: cosine with warmup.
    detailed: |
      A 2026 vision training recipe looks like:

      - **Backbone**: pretrained (CLIP, DINOv2, ImageNet ConvNeXt, etc.).
      - **Head**: small linear + (sometimes) MLP on the backbone features.
      - **Augmentation**: stack of random crop, horizontal flip, colour jitter, Mixup, CutMix, RandAugment, RandomErasing. Modern recipes use 5+ augmentations simultaneously.
      - **Optimizer**: AdamW with weight decay 0.05–0.1 (more than vision used to use).
      - **Schedule**: cosine with warmup (5–10 epochs).
      - **Regularisation**: dropout 0.0–0.1 (often unused), stochastic depth (DropPath) 0.1–0.5.
      - **Mixed precision**: BF16 (or FP16 on older hardware).
      - **Resolution**: 224 default; 384/448 for higher accuracy.

      Training from scratch is rare in 2026. Even a research paper presenting a new architecture usually evaluates by fine-tuning from a strong pretrained checkpoint.
    pitfalls:
      - "Don't bake test-time augmentation into the training augmentation list — they serve different purposes."
  - question: "Where do CNNs still win in 2026?"
    shortAnswer: |
      Edge / mobile deployment (compute and memory efficiency), small-data regimes (CNN inductive bias helps), and applications where translation invariance is essential (medical imaging, satellite, microscopy).
    detailed: |
      CNNs retain advantages over transformers in:

      - **Edge inference**: EfficientNet, MobileNet variants run faster on CPUs and mobile GPUs.
      - **Small data**: the convolution inductive bias means CNNs need less data to reach a given accuracy. Transformers need scale.
      - **Translation-invariant tasks**: medical scans, satellite imagery, microscopy. The spatial homogeneity matches CNN's bias.
      - **Real-time video**: convolutional structure parallelises well; transformers' quadratic attention cost on patches is heavier per frame.
      - **Object detection at high resolution**: YOLO-family models remain CNN-based and dominate real-time detection.

      Transformers win on:
      - **Large-scale image classification** with 100M+ training images.
      - **Image-text models** (CLIP) — naturally handles cross-modal attention.
      - **Long-range dependencies** in images (e.g., scene-level reasoning).

      In 2026 the field accepts both as valid; choose by data scale, deployment target, and downstream task.
    pitfalls:
      - "Hybrid architectures (Swin Transformer, ConvNeXt v2, CoAtNet) blend conv and attention. They often win benchmarks but add complexity."

## The convolution operation

For input X ∈ ℝ^(C_in × H × W) and a learnable kernel K ∈ ℝ^(C_out × C_in × k × k):

`Y[c_out, h, w] = Σ_{c_in, i, j} K[c_out, c_in, i, j] · X[c_in, h+i, w+j] + b[c_out]`

That's a dot product between the kernel and a small patch of the input, summed over input channels. Slide the kernel across all positions; do that for each of C_out filters.

The whole convolution layer has C_out · C_in · k² + C_out parameters. For a 3×3 conv from 64 channels to 128 channels: 73,856 parameters. Compare to a fully connected layer between 64×224×224 activations and 128×224×224 activations: ~1.6 billion parameters. The parameter saving is one of the things that made deep vision tractable.

## Architecture anatomy

A typical CNN block:

```
Input → Conv → BatchNorm → ReLU → Conv → BatchNorm → ReLU → ... → Downsample → ...
```

Repeated with widening channels and shrinking spatial dimensions:

| Layer   | Spatial | Channels | Receptive field |
| ------- | ------- | -------- | --------------- |
| Input   | 224×224 | 3        | 1               |
| Block 1 | 56×56   | 64       | 7               |
| Block 2 | 28×28   | 128      | ~20             |
| Block 3 | 14×14   | 256      | ~70             |
| Block 4 | 7×7     | 512      | ~200            |
| Pool    | 1×1     | 512      | full image      |
| FC      | 1×1     | 1000     | classification  |

Spatial dim halves at each downsampling; channels double. By the end, the feature map is small but rich, and the receptive field covers the input. Global average pooling collapses spatial info into a feature vector, fed to a linear classifier.

## Output shape arithmetic

For a Conv2d with input H × W, kernel size k, stride s, padding p:

Output spatial: H_out = ⌊(H + 2p − k) / s⌋ + 1

For 'same' padding (output size = input size with stride 1): p = (k − 1) / 2. So 3×3 → p=1; 5×5 → p=2; 7×7 → p=3.

For 'valid' (no padding): output shrinks by k − 1 per dimension.

For stride 2 (downsampling): output halves (with appropriate padding).

This arithmetic is the single most common source of shape bugs in vision code. Memorise it.

## What makes a CNN work

Three properties give CNNs their inductive bias for images:

1. **Local connectivity**: each output unit depends on a small patch of the input. Matches natural image statistics (nearby pixels are correlated).
2. **Weight sharing**: the same kernel is applied at every position. A cat detector trained on cats in the top-left works on cats in the bottom-right.
3. **Translation equivariance**: shifting the input shifts the output by the same amount. Matters for object recognition (the object location should not matter, only its presence).

These are _biases_ — assumptions about the task. They help when the task respects them (images), hurt when it doesn't (graphs, sequences, tabular). This is why CNNs dominate vision but not other modalities.

## CNNs vs Vision Transformers in 2026

The competition has settled into roughly:

| Regime                           | Winner                               |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Small data (&lt;100k images)     | CNN — inductive bias helps           |
| Medium data (100k–10M)           | Roughly tied; ConvNeXt vs ViT        |
| Large data (100M+)               | ViT — scales further                 |
| Edge / mobile deployment         | CNN — faster on small devices        |
| Image-text (CLIP, etc.)          | ViT — attention handles cross-modal  |
| Real-time detection (>30 fps)    | CNN (YOLO family)                    |
| Foundation model embeddings      | ViT-based (CLIP, DINOv2, SAM)        |
| Medical / satellite / scientific | CNN — translation invariance is real |

For a 2026 vision project, the default is now "pretrained foundation backbone + small task-specific head," and the foundation backbone is increasingly ViT-based. Pure-from-scratch CNN training is research, not production.

## Common modern tricks

A few specific techniques worth knowing:

- **Depthwise separable convolutions** (MobileNet): factor conv into per-channel + 1×1 pointwise. 8–10× cheaper.
- **Residual connections** (next lesson, A5.3): direct paths between layers; required for depth > 30.
- **Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks**: channel-wise attention; small accuracy boost.
- **Global average pooling**: collapse spatial dim to 1×1 at the end; replaces flatten + FC head.
- **EMA of weights**: maintain an exponential moving average of model weights during training; use EMA for eval and inference. Standard now.
- **Stochastic depth (DropPath)**: randomly skip residual blocks during training. Strong regulariser for deep networks.

Each is a 0.3–1% accuracy gain. The cumulative effect is large; the per-trick gain is small.

## Closing for CNNs

In 2026, CNNs are mature, production-grade, and increasingly outshone at the frontier by transformers. They're still the right tool for:

- Mobile and edge deployment.
- Small-data regimes.
- Real-time detection / segmentation.
- Domains where translation invariance is structural (medical, satellite).
